## 10. Google Ads and Shopping (page 2 of 3)

### April 2023 Coaching Call: Google Shopping, Lieferanten und Pricing-Strategie (Course A) [L0251, 2023]

- [01:46] Example from a member: about 3,800 negative keywords in the Alpha campaign and 1,000 in Beta. Google Shopping goes very broad at first, for example showing a sofa store for "justice sofas" (another brand). Course A says that adding these negatives is correct, and that you should also add terms that only get impressions, before they get clicks. Expect to find maybe 20 to 100 irrelevant terms.
- [05:27] A new product type (for example desks next to an office chair store), 6 to 12 months later: build a separate store branded in a similar way and cross-promote after purchase, instead of mixing products into one store and lowering the conversion rate. Reference: "lead value optimization" retreat talk.
- [08:03] Recession: Course A started high-ticket stores in late 2008, during the recession and real estate collapse, and grew "like crazy". The strategy does not change in a recession. The blueprint says to review ads every two weeks to check they are within KPI. If lower price points convert worse, shift more money and higher CPCs to products at slightly lower price points within the niche.
- [10:39] Spending $2,000 on ads (two months) with no sale means something should have been fixed after the first few hundred dollars. Check keyword quality and negatives first, then the store against competitors.
- [13:13] Returns process: the general policy is 30 days. If the item is not damaged, the customer pays return shipping, unless the supplier covers it. Email the supplier with the customer name and order number and ask for a return label. The supplier emails the label, you forward it to the customer, and the customer ships the item back. You give a refund or store credit, whichever the customer prefers, and the supplier refunds you.
- [21:01] Inventory: supplier stock data can't be automated, because formats differ (weekly Excel email, dealer portal). Keep a closer eye only on suppliers known for stock problems and assume the rest are fine.
- [21:55] A supplier price list with about 20% margin including shipping: negotiate before listing any products, because 20% is not enough after costs.
- [23:50] Distributors: use them only when the brand has no direct program and routes all online retailers through one distribution company. Contact the distributor anyway, because it sometimes has MAP policies for that brand.
- [28:37] The module 1 video of 8 to 10 product categories (camping gear, power washers etc.) is still viable (2023). The blog's top-10 niche list has been updated every year for 9 years, and any niche from any year is usable.
- [29:31] There is no fixed Alpha vs Beta spend percentage. It depends on daily search volume, so set up per blueprint and read the split after a few weeks of consistent traffic.
- [32:59] Supplier approval expectations from a minimum list of 20: about 50% (10 of 20) is typical early, 8 of 20 is possible, 15 of 20 is a great start. Aim for at least half before launch, but a little under half is OK. 0 of 20 after actually reaching them means change the approach: switch from phone to email, or find the small company's owner on LinkedIn and message them there.
- [33:49] Done-for-you content marketing: start it once ads are running with traffic and ideally a few sales, so content can be built around what already gets traffic and sells ("put gasoline on the fire"). Example member: 3 approved suppliers.
- [36:35] Large and heavy free-shipping example: a $200 futon with $80 wholesale still loses because shipping "crushes you". Find the average shipping cost by entering fake shipments on freightcenter.com, then promote only products whose wholesale to MAP spread covers free shipping.
- [39:50] A product that keeps getting disapproved in Merchant Center (for example 3 of 100): troubleshoot first, then contact Merchant Center support. If it still can't be fixed, remove it from the product feed rather than leaving it, because it won't show anyway.
- [42:26] For small non-freight items when the supplier won't ship on its account: open a free account on ups.com or fedex.com and send the account number to the supplier. The supplier creates shipments on your behalf, and you get billed separately by the supplier (product) and UPS/FedEx (shipping).
- [45:28] Demo or placeholder products from licensable Google Images have no price or description: make them up. Use the niche's average price (for example $2,000) and a basic description, for example "This bamboo standup desk is 76 inches wide, supports 200 pounds".
- [46:18] Phone number: a visible phone number raises trust even subconsciously, and most callers call to place orders. MightyCall (US); OpenPhone for members abroad.
- [48:58] Remarketing: target all site visitors and exclude only people who have placed orders.
- [49:52] Discounts and MAP: suppliers don't need to approve coupon codes or promotions unless the agreement forbids discounts for that brand. The listed price must be MAP, and a code may lower the price only after it is entered. Giveaways or bonus gifts have never needed supplier approval.
- [50:48] As soon as a supplier says it will send the agreement, stop everything else, fill it out and send it back right away.
- [55:22] An unknown Search Console error (for example "alternative page with canonical tag"): paste the exact message in quotes into Google and look for forum or Reddit threads where others solved it.
- [57:02] Email: Course A uses Klaviyo. Don't cancel an existing email tool before reaching the Klaviyo lessons. Export the list and templates into Klaviyo, then cancel. Possibly outdated: the April 2025 call says Shopify Email is enough.
- [57:51] build.com is a retailer, not a brand supplier. Course A never found a brand owned by it, so at most look at a price list.
- [1:01:38] Store readiness before contacting suppliers, besides removing the password: use the real domain (not the default Shopify URL), make every link work (for example the footer "About us" must not be empty), and replace or remove the default social links, which point to Shopify. The site should look as it will at launch, so after approval you only upload products.
- [1:02:27] Resale or sales tax certificate: Google "[state] resale certificate" (also called a sales tax certificate) and use only the state's .gov site, because other sites charge for it. These are usually online forms that take about 5 minutes and give you a number to send to suppliers.
- [1:03:20] Course A does not recommend selling on Amazon, and most suppliers don't allow it anyway.
- [1:05:58] Phone script when a caller wants a discount on a MAP product (example: 5 office chairs, asking 15% off): "The price is set at the lowest we are able to sell this at. That is industry-wide, an agreement every retailer has with the manufacturer, to protect the brand." Then offer speed instead. If it ships the same business day when ordered before noon (for example a call at 11 AM), promise a tracking number by the evening. If the call comes at 3 PM, it ships tomorrow. Learn the supplier's cutoff times in advance.
- [1:07:44] A brand that sells direct with a great online store: usually skip it. Exception: it sells at MSRP (example $2,000) while retailers may sell at MAP (example $1,500).
- [1:12:07] A MAP supplier with only about 25% margin: keep the products listed (the price guarantee can stay because prices are MAP), but stop ads to that supplier and sell only through organic and free Google listings.
- [1:13:00] "600 styles": 600 products in a niche is fine. If they are 600 sub-niches, check which sub-niche still meets the module 1 and 2 criteria and specialise there; if none does, niche up.
- [1:13:55] Non-US residents using Shopify Payments: Course A knows plenty of people doing this successfully (the shutdown reports likely had other causes). Any processor other than Shopify Payments adds Shopify's transaction fee shown on its pricing page, about 1% and lower on higher plans.
- [1:15:39] Course A does not currently work with Canadian suppliers (2023, US business).
- [1:15:39] Search text campaigns, in Course A's experience, convert at a lower rate and cost more than Google Shopping, so ROAS is lower. Build them only after Shopping data shows which product name, brand name and SKU combinations bring volume and ROAS.
- [1:16:31] Zapier Premium was $50/month in 2023 (it used to be $29).
- [1:17:23] A supplier price list with 500 SKUs might lead you to upload about 100. Do not upload products under $200 or with low margins.
- [1:18:19] Only one supplier photo per product: short term, if the image is high resolution, crop or zoom into details (armrest, material) to make several images from one. Long term, find industry trade shows on tsn.com (TSNN) within the next year where your suppliers exhibit, and shoot your own photos and videos with a phone.
- [1:20:01] UPS Store address over a USPS box: it gives a suite number instead of a PO box number.
- [1:20:53] Shop Pay Installments had about a $1,000 limit per customer in beta, raised later. Use another financing option only if the AOV and almost all products are above Shop Pay's limit; otherwise "Shop Pay is the best".
- [1:23:29] Shipping: when possible, have suppliers ship on their own accounts (much easier). Course A can't recall a supplier ever adding a big upcharge for this.
- [1:24:23] Assembly upcharge (for example a swing set): use Bold Product Options as an add-on at checkout. For freight, curbside delivery is the cheapest level.
- [1:25:15] "Recommended products" on product pages: only use add-ons (for example a hiking backpack, with straps in a chosen colour or a waterproof cover).
- [1:26:58] Spec and comparison tables for high-ticket products: build them manually (designers, using code found by searching "comparison table Shopify"), not with an app. Keep apps to the ones that really help, because apps can slow the store.
- [1:29:35] Launch timing: 3 to 4 weeks to launch is not too long for suppliers. 100 products upload "like nothing"; a niche with 3,000 SKUs takes longer. To 80/20 it, upload first the top-listed products from each approved supplier, using the order in top competitors' category pages, so every supplier is live by launch. Add the rest later.
- [1:31:24] Entity and DBA structure: "VO Stores LLC" holds the bank account, the Shopify business information and the supplier forms, and does business as greenhouseplus.com and modernchairsplus.com.
- [1:32:18] Extra items from a supplier outside the niche (for example sinks for a countertop store): add them only if it makes sense logically and the top competitors sell them as bundles or add-ons. Example of no: a standup desk brand that also makes TV consoles.
- [1:34:00] A supplier at 20% margin: no ads to its products. Spend most of your time contacting other suppliers (niche up a level if needed) and put ad spend on suppliers with healthier margins.
- [1:35:46] Affiliate example: an online drum school sells an intro pack of four 30-minute lessons for $300 and pays the affiliate $100 per $300 sale through a unique link.
- [1:37:03] Budget: the YouTube video "Realistic dropshipping budget for 2023" covers the high and low ends of launch costs. Launching costs less than many people assume.
- [1:38:52] The GA4 migration lesson 6.1.x is about 30 minutes. New stores can skip it, because all other lessons already use GA4.
- [1:45:26] Suppliers that deliver only to mainland addresses: large and heavy items to Alaska and Hawaii cost about as much as international shipping. Exclude Alaska and Hawaii from the Shopify shipping zones so no one can order there.
- [1:46:20] Return policy with mixed suppliers: the default policy is the one most suppliers share (example: 8 of 10 have the standard 30 days). Brands with very different terms (no returns, or 1-year returns) get their own policy in the product page "Shipping and returns" tab and an asterisk exclusion at the bottom of the general policy page.
- [1:46:20] Done-for-you store build can start as soon as module 2 is finished. Mentoring for niche selection can start right away.
- [1:48:08] Shopify pricing fields: compare-at = MSRP (the struck-through everyday price), price = MAP, cost = wholesale. Calculate margin between wholesale and MAP, never MSRP. If the supplier's own site sells below MAP, don't list the products and don't drive traffic to them. Example: MSRP $2,000, supplier site $1,800, MAP $1,500 is fine.
- [1:48:08] The inventory or availability field on a product: any number, for example 100. Stock isn't tracked through it.
- [1:49:56] Collection naming: name the collection literally after what it contains (for example "bamboo-standup-desks"), without overthinking long-tail keywords. For extra description keywords, use Google related searches (for example "sit stand", "converter", "bamboo desk"), skipping brand names you don't sell, such as Jarvis. Check what the top 2 organic results include on their pages.
- [1:52:40] Reviews widget not showing: use the app's Get help or Get support button in Shopify, which works even on free plans, and grant the developer access (no password needed).
- [1:53:32] Module 6 had about 30 lessons on traffic sources in 2023: free Google, Google Shopping, Google remarketing, Meta, Bing.
- [1:54:26] Price where online buying gets harder: Course A used to say $1,000, now (2023) about $1,500 to $2,000. Above that, expect pre-sale support (calls and questions).
- [1:55:19] Product uploads: do the first ones yourself. Record the next 10 or so with Loom (Chrome extension) while doing them your way, then hire on Upwork. Pick someone with experience in that exact job and good reviews, give a test project of about 20 products, then extend. Rate for someone good in the Philippines: about $5 to $8/hour (2023). Course A won't offer a done-for-you upload service, because supplier data formats (Excel, dealer login, website) can't be standardised.
- [1:57:08] Ads show whether they convert within 2 to 3 days, because they reach people already deciding where to buy. Low end is $5/day; Course A's own new stores typically start at about $30/day. Expect profit or conversions very early, not after months.
- [1:58:52] Reason to keep related stores in one entity (with one EIN): when selling, related stores (even indirect competitors) are sold together as a bundle, so mixed funds are fine. Unrelated stores get new entities so finances don't mix.
- [1:59:48] Mock store before suppliers: one image and a short description per product is enough.
- [2:00:40] Customer service: answering the phone or calling back has brought, Course A estimates, multiple millions of dollars in sales over the years ("I can't believe somebody answered").
- [2:01:34] Reply quickly even with bad news; about 24 business hours is a reasonable timeframe. "Bad news is better than no news." Delayed replies that make customers think nobody is there cause real customer service problems. Example: a customer who bought 5 office chairs for $8,000 and whose shipment slipped.
- [2:02:32] Margin math at 20% (supplier pays shipping): minus the 2.9% card fee and up to 10% ad spend, about 7.1% is left. That is too thin to run ads; it might work with free traffic only.
- [2:08:40] Separate one product: take it out of the main Alpha and Beta, and build a new Alpha and Beta with their own shared budget and their own negative keywords, containing only that product (no three campaigns sharing one budget).
- [2:11:25] Non-resident incorporating with Stripe Atlas (incorporation plus EIN takes about 4 to 6 weeks): wait for the EIN before contacting suppliers so you can keep the conversation moving. Meanwhile, study the niche in module 4 and module 5 (competitor weaknesses).
- [2:13:59] Mindset lesson ("Mindset, use this for best results") is about 45 minutes. Make the goal concrete, for example an extra $10,000 a month net within 12 months, for paying off debt, buying a first home, leaving the job, or 3 months in Europe next summer.
- [2:14:53] Bank: online vs physical branch doesn't matter, but after the 2023 bank failures maybe choose a bigger bank name.
- [2:20:55] Expert installation for high-value products: ask local installers or contractors what referral incentive they already offer, then build the deal around it. Offering installation raises conversion but adds manual work. The alternative is a product page notice that installation needs, for example, an electrician.
- [2:22:41] Integrately is another Zapier alternative, at $19.99/month (2023).
- [2:25:25] Average-price test: if the organic Google Shopping average is under $200 (for example office chairs at $180 organic vs $1,000 in sponsored ads), add a modifier such as "modern office chairs" to see whether organic listings rise (example: about $600).
- [2:27:11] Pivot inside the niche instead of starting over, for example drop standup desks that turn out too complex and sell "modern desks" instead.
- [2:27:11] Products sold by the store are not rebranded. Customers expect separate brand boxes and tracking numbers, like a Sony TV and a Sonos soundbar ordered from bestbuy.com arriving days apart. Damage is covered by shipping insurance and the item is replaced.
- [2:28:55] Turning an existing China dropshipping store (about 200 and 150 products) into this model: keep the store and name if you like the layout, wipe all current products, and replace them with brand products from a related niche that meets modules 1 and 2.
- [2:31:35] Suppliers saying "we're not taking new stores for 2023" or "come back at X traffic": focus on anyone with any positive response, get real products live from even 1 or 2 suppliers, and consider broadening the niche slightly.
- [2:32:27] One niche means one product type, not one product. Example: standup paddle boards with 20 dropship-friendly brands, so 20 suppliers and possibly hundreds of products.
- [2:34:25] Click threshold detail: 100 unique clicks is too small to judge a 2.5% rate. Course A is willing to go up to about 500 clicks on a product to judge its conversion rate. At 300 unique clicks with no sales, adjust something.
- [2:35:21] Suppliers that charge per-pallet and drop-ship fees when shipping on their account: get sizes and weights of the most popular products, take free freightcenter.com quotes from the supplier's warehouse to different parts of the country, and know your margin before any sale.
- [2:36:14] A supplier asking for a "business license": send your state tax ID. States call it different things.
- [2:44:47] Copy-protection apps (disabling right-click) aren't worth using, because copiers get around them. If someone copies your content, file a DMCA takedown with their web host.
- [2:46:39] Merchant Center problem while still uploading: keep uploading products to Shopify, but stop sending new products to Merchant Center by disconnecting the product feed or turning off auto-submit in the Feed for Google Shopping app until it is resolved.
- [2:49:24] A Canadian supplier without US-stocked inventory isn't worth it for a US-focused store. A supplier that does not enforce MAP is not automatically a no (see "How to profit with non-MAP suppliers").
- [2:53:52] Inventory checks cover only the best-selling 20% of products from suppliers known to have had low stock before. Ask suppliers to email you about new product releases so you can add them right away.
- [2:53:52] Tone on supplier calls: mix professionalism and energy. Sound confident and happy to offer their products; neither has to be over the top.
- [2:55:40] Supplier with no formal agreement (often small family companies): not a deal breaker. You only need, at least in email, how returns work and what the price policy is (MAP or not).
- [2:57:29] Backorder note wording (expected ship date): example "April 12th, 2023".

### Attribution und Conversion Tracking für Google Ads (Course B) [L0256, 2021-22]

- [01:19] Digital ads can reach the whole country for $10/day, $20/day or $1,000/month, compared with tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars for pre-digital national campaigns.
- [08:16] Under-reporting leads to conservative assumptions, which leads to bids that are too low or too little spend and missed opportunities. Being conservative is still safer than overestimating, because overestimating leads to overspending.
- [14:27] Bread example (Oct 1 to Nov 15, 2020): checkout.getbread.com/referral shows 6 users (0 new) but 3 sales worth about $5,500. Nobody discovers products through a checkout, so these sales belong to Google Ads or Google organic.
- [17:33] Worked reallocation example: direct/none had 14 conversions and about $30,000. Assigning half to the $73,000 Google Ads revenue adds about $15,000 for a total of about $88,000, which gives a higher ROAS and justifies more spend. The abandoned-cart email ($4,500) and Bread (about $5,000) also belong to front-end sources. In total about $35,500 to $40,000 needs reattributing, of which about 72% goes to Google Ads (its share of new users).
- [11:38] The stock GA Source/Medium report (Acquisition) gives a high-level snapshot of all traffic sources. Medium separates paid (cpc) from organic for the same source (Google, Bing, Facebook).
- [24:16] Revenue per user is "not particularly useful" in the custom report. The same custom-report method can also be filtered to google/organic to track organic traffic.
