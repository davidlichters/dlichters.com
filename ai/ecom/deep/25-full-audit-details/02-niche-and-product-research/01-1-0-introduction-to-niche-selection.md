## 02. Niche and Product Research (page 1 of 3)

### 1.0 Introduction To Niche Selection (Dokument) (Course A) [L0009, undated]

- (document, no timestamp) Good niche = lots of sales, low amount of customer service, high profit per sale (suits semi-automated sales). Bad niche = no sales or, worse, lots of problematic sales, lots of customer service (pre-sale effort and post-sale follow-up), small margins.
- (document, no timestamp) "Niche selection can make or break your business. Do NOT rush the process." Many niches can be profitable, but that does not mean you should enter all of them.
- (document, no timestamp) Every niche from the 500 High Ticket Niche Ideas list must be validated individually with Module 1 and Module 2 criteria; questions go to the course Discord community.

### 1.0 Introduction To Niche Selection Niche Research Worksheet (Dokument) (Course A) [L0010, undated]

- (document, no timestamp) Goal of Module 1: a list of 50 niche ideas stored in the Niche Research Worksheet.
- (document, no timestamp) Worksheet setup: Google Drive template -> File -> Make a copy -> rename, e.g. "YourName - Course A Research"; or Excel download (Excel or Apple Numbers).
- (document, no timestamp) In Module 1 use only Column A of the sheet titled "Niche Research Worksheet". Ignore all other columns (average price, profit, demographic, brand loyalty, etc.) and the other sheets in the workbook until later modules.

### 1.1 Pricing Criteria (Dokument) (Course A) [L0012, undated]

- (doc, no timestamp) Brainstorm method: look around your own environment (home, office, gym, daily life) and list anything typically priced above $200; ignore personal passion, only price matters at this stage.
- (doc) Fewer customers also means fewer tracking numbers and fewer potential issues: 1,000 low-ticket customers vs 10 happy high-ticket customers for the same profit.

### 1.2 Product Size Weight Profit (Dokument) (Course A) [L0016, undated]

- (doc) All Course A stores offer free shipping; described as the industry standard for almost 20 years. Customer pays $0, shipping comes out of the margin.
- (doc) Net profit benchmark ~30%: "sometimes 25%, sometimes higher, but 30% is the benchmark".
- (doc) Large/heavy products often cost the same to ship whether cheap or expensive.
- (doc) $200 minimum applies to small parcel-sized items shipped as UPS/FedEx packages (vs $1,000+ for freight).
- (doc) Worksheet refinement examples: espresso machines kept with a note to verify pricing later; new niches added from price/margin logic: laser engraving machines, CNC machines, swing sets, backyard playgrounds.

### 1.3 Target Demographic (Dokument) (Course A) [L0018, undated]

- (doc) Upper middle class buyers tend to have fewer complaints and fewer returns (in addition to less pre-selling).
- (doc) Rugs example: "Rugs" is too broad because it covers $30 rugs and $500+ rugs and appeals to all demographics; move such a broad niche to the bottom of the list and do not research it further, keep it only for reference.

### 1.3 Target Market (Dokument) (Course A) [L0019, undated]

- (doc) Customers to avoid: those who haggle all day, complain about everything, or call for daily updates. Wanted customers: place the order online, calmly wait for delivery, then send a thank-you email. Possibly outdated wording (Blueprint, author cites "past ten years").
- (doc) Upper middle class may be single people or families; goal phrased as "quality products at fair pricing to upper-middle-class households".

### 1.5 Passion Vs. Profit (Dokument) (Course A) [L0025, undated]

- [document, no timestamp, year not shown] Example "Johnny", a Course A member since 2013: his passion was scuba diving, but he chose not to build his store around it; he sold better opportunities (products that were easier to sell and more profitable) and used the profit to fund his scuba diving hobby.

### AdLib Produktrecherche & Scaling Q&A (Course C) [L0209, 2023]

- [19:15] For products whose ads make promises (pain removal, straightening legs and similar), either skip them or test them only in a separate, newly bought ad account so you do not risk your main business manager.
- [16:37] Niche signal: in the live AdLib session, a large share of the first eight ads found were kids toys (several from the same store); many advertisers in one category in the top results is an indicator of a niche worth testing (Sept to Christmas window).
- [07:11] Twitter ads: a student reported getting banned instantly; the mentor had never tried Twitter ads (planned for 2024), only Facebook and TikTok ads.
- [05:22] Page-builder order used by the mentor: start with the PageFly team; if PageFly gives no results, try the Course C teacher's team (named in the transcript).
- [11:15] AdLib usage: filter to Shopify stores and leave call-to-action and other filters open; the percentage shown is the growth in the number of ads the advertiser runs.

### Amazon FBA: Produkte privatlabeln und mit dem Amazon-Netzwerk skalieren (Course A) [L0228, 2015]

- [28:02-29:41] Cheap-product comparison: a $11.97 product with $1 unit cost, $1 shipping and $1.80 Amazon fees has $8.17 gross profit (69%) but only about $6 net before ads after reordering; at about $1 per Amazon ad click (own products about $1.30) even a 20% ad conversion rate leaves only about $2, and at 10% you probably lose money. Own product ($14.55 net) breaks even if 1 of 14 ad clickers buys. (2015)
- [37:12-38:48] Supplier payment terms: pay about 30% deposit, release the remaining 70% only after the supplier sends photographic proof that the finished, branded product is ready to ship; pay by wire/bank transfer, never Western Union; a good supplier sends a professional purchase order listing deposit, balance, logo and shipping costs. (2015)
- [41:13-42:03] Amazon has five bullet points: put your two best benefits in the first and the last bullet, because scanners read first and last. Give the product description its own bold title (problem-solving or benefit) to catch scanners; only basic HTML (bold, hyphens), no images or real bullet lists. (2015)
- [39:32] Build and optimize the Amazon product page while the supplier is producing (during the 2 to 3 week wait). (2015)
- [57:19-58:06] Press releases: the presenters went from 3 per week to 7 per week (daily) and saw no difference for their products; test it, it reportedly works mainly for supplement sellers. (2015)
- [58:58] Google/Bing ads to the Amazon listing: shopping ads converted better than text ads, Bing converted better than Google for their products, but both far below Amazon sponsored product ads. (2015)
- [59:47-1:02:11] Tomoson (transcribed "Thomason") blogger reviews: bloggers post on blog, Facebook, Instagram, Twitter within 30 days of getting the product; presenters set a $197 price for a $40 product (to avoid free-giveaway reviews not sticking) and had to pay bloggers $2 via PayPal; 50% never wrote anything; all reviews that came were 5-star; quality bloggers cost $100 to $300 each. (2015)
- [1:03:08] Beware screenshots of e.g. 200 units on day two: usually a "blasting service" giving the item away via coupon codes at $0.99; check the average sales per unit. (2015)
- [1:04:09-1:06:40] Case study details: in weeks 5 to 6 about $5,000 sold in two weeks and $3,500 paid out by Amazon (bi-weekly direct deposit); on June 1, 2015 the price was raised to $35.95 and still got 12 orders that day; within six weeks the initial investment was paid back plus enough for a reorder; profit taken from the week 7 to 8 payout. Presenter calls it "solid" but not a home run; his second product did better. (2015)
- [47:31-49:57] Competitor listing examples: one with a single photo and minimal bullets at BSR about 1,500 sells about 60 to 80 units per day; one at BSR 3,600 sells about 30 to 40 per day; a listing with almost no description sells about 1 per day. (2015)
- [50:40] Switching bullet points to double-star markers raised their conversion rate by 5% (not guaranteed for everyone); e-book covers made on Fiverr for about $20. (2015)
- [1:12:59] Deliberate stock-out test: running out of inventory made the listing "drop really quick". (2015)
- [11:58] Even with FBA, create a Facebook page for branding so the business looks reputable; expect only a few customer emails. (2015)
- [12:51] Alternative FBA model: retail arbitrage (buy discounted products at e.g. Walmart, resell on Amazon at about double); presenters prefer private label for the lifestyle. (2015)
- [46:08] Promotional codes inside the Amazon package/listing can point buyers to your own dropshipping store (e.g. a 15% off code). (2015)
- [1:19:29] It cost about $50 extra (whole order) to sew their logo onto every mesh bag; at least 8 sellers sold the identical resistance bands, competitors at $24.97 vs their $35 to $39.97, and theirs was about second best of the eight by sales thanks to perceived value. Adding two extra handles (about $0.60) could justify $3 to $5 more. (2015)
- [1:28:45-1:29:36] Quality control: ship the first (small) shipment to yourself using Amazon's partnered shipping on their account (about $50), inspect, then forward to Amazon; or pay a third-party inspection service in China (before shipping) or in the US (before Amazon). (2015)
- [1:23:00] Order fewer units of a product you love rather than more of a cheaper product you are 50-50 on; when stock runs fast, raise the price ("jack that price up"). (2015)
- [32:48-33:37] Supplier lead times on Alibaba can be 30, 45 or 60 days before shipping; sea freight adds 30 to 45 days; presenters always ship by air for now; switch to sea for oversized items or orders of 5,000 to 10,000 units when selling 30 to 50 per day. (2015)

### April 2021 Coaching Call: Lieferanten, Google Ads und Shop-Setup (Course A) [L0247, 2021]

- [16:11] Audit trend rule: a single small fluctuation is fine and randomness is normal, but if you see e.g. a 5% or 10% move the wrong way one week and another 5% or 10% the next week, watch the trend and go into the ad accounts to fix things; the teacher's team audits weekly, new store owners every two weeks, established owners also weekly. [2021]
- [32:46] Goal setting example: instead of yearly income goals, set a 90-day goal such as $2,000, $2,500 or $3,000 net profit per month within three months; results after a year range from $0 to hundreds of thousands net profit, so no average is given. [2021]
- [53:37 / 1:03:03] An attendee's average profit per product of $100 to $180 in the niches being evaluated was answered with "absolutely" enough to be successful (contrast: other lessons use a $200 minimum profit; possibly inconsistent). [2021]
- [58:44] Promotions cadence: typically one promotion per month; in July the teacher runs a 4th of July sale lasting 3 to 5 days; he has never run "Black Friday in July". [2021]
- [1:15:20-1:16:15] Australian (.com.au) domains: if Shopify does not sell one, buy the domain at any registrar (the teacher used namecheap.com for years before buying through Shopify Domains) and link it to Shopify. [2021]
- [1:26:13 / 2:43:29] The EIN is free to get online on the IRS website, does not take long, and practically every supplier asks for it, so get it early. [2021]
- [1:29:39] All retailers should get the same MAP; if a competitor sells below the MAP on your price list, send the supplier a screenshot and simply ask what MAP is and why that company is not selling at it. [2021]
- [1:30:32] Australian members mostly build stores selling in Australia (five or six years earlier most built US stores from Australia). [2021]
- [1:47:05] UPS and FedEx Ground: the best prices are usually on the supplier's shipping accounts because of their volume; with your own account, discounts come as your shipping volume grows. [2021]
- [1:48:44] A supplier charging a "service fee" for licensing: check whether the margins still work with the fee included; if the brand has MAP and margins hold with the fee, it is fine. [2021]
- [1:48:59-1:49:49] Branding in year one and two: "minimum viable effort"; too much time on image, style, persona and logo early does not make money; prioritise what makes money and gives customers good experiences, build the brand later once profits and a team exist. [2021]
- [1:55:16] Profitable established stores sell for hundreds of thousands up to millions of dollars; the teacher sells his stores privately (not through the course). [2021]
- [2:05:01-2:05:56] Naming tips for store/domain: product type in the domain is nice but not necessary; memorable; no "cute" misspellings (people forget them); no hyphen; always a .com; shorter is better, not three words long. [2021]
- [2:09:07] Teacher's entity structure: each network of related stores/businesses runs through one LLC taxed as an S-corp, with the stores operated as DBAs ("doing business as") under it; ask an accountant for specifics. [2021]
- [2:15:33-2:16:21, 2:52:58] Installation-heavy niches: the teacher would not do one as a first store; finding local installers in every town is a headache; prefer a nationwide assembly/installation company recommended by the supplier, even at a slightly smaller margin. [2021]
- [2:18:08] Google search text ads: not profitable at the start; the teacher considered a bonus lesson for stores making $50,000+ per month. [2021]
- [2:23:34] Company name examples: owner's initials plus "Enterprises", "Management" or "Stores" all work; the name appears on bank statements and supplier approval forms. [2021]
- [2:29:42] Product description generators: not recommended at the start; use the supplier's descriptions, then rewrite the descriptions of products that get impressions and clicks. [2021]
- [2:35:16] Non-US residents can open a US store; members from all over the world have done it. [2021]
- [2:39:26] Do not make a copy of the store while getting approvals: as soon as the first supplier approves, remove the demo products and upload the real ones; ads do not need to be turned on right away. [2021]
- [2:40:14] Domain prices: never pay e.g. $65,000 for a taken domain; the most the teacher ever paid was $5,000 (for a domain he never used); typical cost about $14/year; pick an available variant. [2021]
- [2:42:40] Using the supplier's description first and changing it later still helps SEO, because the sitemap submitted to Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools makes them recrawl changed pages. [2021]
- [2:49:41] Assembly/white-glove service: if the customer needs it, charge it before the order is confirmed (e.g. product-page add-on via Bold Product Options); offering optional assembly after the sale in the confirmation email is untested by the teacher. [2021]
- [2:52:07] Return process: customer requests a return, you notify the supplier, the supplier sends a return shipping label, the customer ships it back, the supplier refunds you and you refund the customer; return/refund policy template is in Module 5 "Content Page Templates". [2021]
- [2:53:47] Demo products: make up the prices (and generic names with a few random stock photos); checkout is not enabled, nobody buys them. [2021]
- [2:47:59] Coaching calls: first Wednesday of every month, 2 p.m. Eastern; replay in fastbusinessforum.com under "Q&A coaching calls with Course A" the same evening. [2021]
- [1:39:14] 2021 retreat in Portugal (Lisbon): September 2, 3 and 4, plus optional VIP day on the 5th; early bird tickets; refund or transfer if cancelled by COVID. [2021]
- [2:26:57] Teacher's podcast "eCommerce Lifestyle": new episodes Monday and Thursday mornings, archive at ecommercelifestyle.com/episodes. [2021]
