## General (lessons whose topics have no chapter) (page 5 of 7)

### Breakthrough Advertising (Dokument) (Course C) [L0330, undated]

- Framework: the book's "Seven Basic Techniques of Breakthrough Copy" for body copy: (1) Intensification, (2) Identification, (3) Gradualization, (4) Redefinition, (5) Mechanization, (6) Concentration, (7) Camouflage; plus "Final Touches": Verification, Reinforcement, Interweaving, momentum builders, mood/sincerity. Three dimensions of the prospect's mind map to them: Desire (Intensification), Identification, Belief (Gradualization through Camouflage).
- Identification: two kinds of roles a buyer wants to express. Character roles (adjectives: "progressive", "chic", "well-read"), usually only hinted at, untestable, therefore easily believed when shown as a non-verbal image symbol. Achievement roles (nouns/titles: "Executive", "Home Owner", "Good Mother", "Civic Leader") must be displayed through products.
- Identification: a product serves a role in three ways: helps achieve mastery of the role (book on philosophy), speeds/simplifies mastery (speed-reading course), and symbolises the mastery for others (the bookshelf). "At least half of all purchases made today cannot be understood in terms of function alone" (example: a $5,000, 150-mph sports car driven on 35-mph parkways).
- Identification decides the sale when products perform the same job and price is no longer a factor.
- The most general roles Americans buy: virile or feminine; exciting, unique, adventurous; friendly and well-liked; important, influential and correct; modern and up-to-date; above all successful.
- Primary image rule: you cannot replace a product's existing (primary) image; subordinate it and use it as the bridge to more favourable images. Case: Chesterfield 1926 "Blow some my way": man kept in the picture but darkened, woman as focal point. Case: hypothetical piston-ring ad built around a Mercedes SL-300 ($12,000 sports car), precise mechanic, racing owner, rings installed as performance equipment, not repair.
- Believability check for any image, two questions: (1) Do people already believe the product has these traits, and can they identify their present or next-step-up life with them? (2) If not, which primary image must serve as a "believability bridge"? Case: deodorant soap using an elite society image for a mass market failed and the disbelief spread to the performance claims.
- Physical product image sources for identification: its appearance; its components and structure; its technical background.
- Gradualization (belief): if you violate the prospect's established beliefs "in the slightest degree" nothing can save the ad; channel belief behind one claim. Build a chain of acceptances from facts he already accepts to the facts you need. It determines the ad's structure, not its content.
- Gradualization opening devices: (1) Inclusion question (Yes-train, e.g. "Ask yourself these nine questions" for The Art of Selfishness); (2) Detailed identification (list his symptoms/problems exactly, research first); (3) Contradiction of present false beliefs, only with strong authority (cosmetics by a plastic surgeon: "forget everything you have heard about what age must do"); (4) Language of logic ("The reason is clear", "Here's why", "Therefore", "This has been proved by thousands", "They discovered, in case after case"); (5) Syllogistic thinking (spark-plug ad: bigger spark = bigger explosion = more power and mileage); (6) other belief forms: contingency structures (If...then; Was your...then), echoing repetition of proof ("These experts found..." x3), promise-proof-promise alternation, paragraph parallelism.
- Redefinition (removing objections): three drawback categories: too complicated, not important enough (market too narrow), costs too much. Classic flip: Lifebuoy soap in the 1930s turned its medicinal odor into proof of power via the "B.O." campaign. Simplification case: TV repair book failed with "Do your own TV repairs" headline; the winning version talked about breakdowns and expenses first and redefined repairs as minor adjustments. For a genuinely easier product: (1) redefine, (2) mechanize the simplicity; "innovation without acceptance is valueless".
- Mechanization: the reader makes three demands while reading: more information/image ("tell me more"), proof ("who says so?"), mechanism ("how does it work?"). How much mechanism depends on awareness. Stage 1: just name a known mechanism and compete on price (camera ad example: three named features, "Only $135"). Stage 2: describe it (Promise, then Reason Why; Rinso 1926 "Who else wants a whiter wash, with no hard work?"). Stage 3: if the mechanism is strong or the market sophisticated, sell the mechanism itself, even in the headline ("Floats fat right out of your body", "Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery").
- Mechanization for price cuts: an unexplained price reduction is not believed and produces only a fraction of its sales power; give the mechanism (reason why) for the bargain. Case: Robert Collier "Before the Price Goes Up!" letter, an overstocked Madras mill, a quarter of a million yards bought below cost.
- Concentration (attacking alternatives): five competitive weapons: superiority of product, superiority of promise, product role, response/reaction (escalate claims, shift mechanisms, new markets), and direct attack. Use direct attack mainly when your budget is much smaller than the leader's or most prospects are already his customers; ignore competitors when you dominate. Rule: "Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time"; the attack must be shown to be for the prospect's good. Case: "fire injectors" vs spark plugs ("Mechanics and engineers read this carefully"), later reinforced by "the U.S. Air Force pays premium prices for surface supported injectors".
- Camouflage (borrowing believability from the medium): (1) format: copy the publication's own editorial look; a single format change "can add 50% to your readership, and your results". Case: a book ad on handling people adapted feature-for-feature for the Wall Street Journal (journal-type headline, two stacked old-fashioned subheads, bars around the first subhead, subheads at far left, line drawing instead of photo) was repeated 19 times, once a month, with no drop-off; the house-set version on 12 to 15 media was only mildly successful. (2) phraseology: news style with dateline/by-line ("Skin specialist demonstrates how to rinse away your blackheads, By Claire Hoffman, New York"); in direct mail imitate mail that is always opened (refund checks, government letters, dividend notices, confidential reports, high-priced newsletters); in radio/TV imitate news format and sound. (3) mood: understatement (Volkswagen ads: few adjectives, short sentences, self-deprecating).
- Verification (proof placement): proof is most effective where the reader unconsciously demands it; the same statistic is dull early and dramatic at the moment the reader is searching for a solution ("1 + 1 can often equal 10", "Position increases power"). Distinction: Documentation = outside proof (stats, tests); Mechanization = logical verbal proof; Verification = arranging documentation in the copy.
- Momentum builders, two types: (1) momentum phrases in transition sentences ("Here's how", "Let me explain", "For example", "And yet it's only the beginning", "Just wait till you try this"); (2) incomplete statements/teasers: create interest in a point, raise a question about it, imply the answer comes later.
- Guarantee-as-climax example with numbers (Technique 13): spark plugs guaranteed 2 full years; first month must give up to 9 more miles per gallon and up to 31 more horsepower or full refund; if performance fades within 2 years, a new set free. Plant-food example: $2.98 for 144 pellets, 1 to 2 pellets per plant.
- Case-history example (Technique 6): reducing-plan ad with "as much as five pounds a week", total losses of 17, 25 and 34 pounds, "turned back their weight clock 5, 10 and even 15 years"; rose ad comparing against roses selling "as high as $3.75 for a single plant".
- History notes: patent-medicine copywriters of the 1890s practised desire intensification; Claude Hopkins moved it into general advertising before World War I; it dominated advertising until 1954, after which identification became the big merchandising shift.
- Copywriter's library recommended by Schwartz: Julian Watkins, The 100 Greatest Advertisements (1949); Claude Hopkins, My Life in Advertising (1927); John E. Kennedy, Intensive Advertising; The Lasker Story; Robert Collier Letter Book; David Ogilvy, Confessions of an Advertising Man; Rosser Reeves, Reality in Advertising; Milton Biow, Butting In; plus Caples, Young, Glim, Bedell, Dichter and Martineau (motivation research), Baker, Visual Persuasion (layout).
- Foreword facts: book sold used for over $900 before reissue; Schwartz's earlier books: How to Double Your Child's Grades in School (1964) and How to Double Your Power to Learn; he traded copywriting for access to mailing lists to promote his own books.

### ca hvertising (Dokument) (Course C) [L0340, undated]

#### Chapter 1 / Principles 1-17
- p.19: Daniel Starch (born 1883, Starch Advertising Readership Reports) found that ads showing factories, staff, square footage and equipment were ignored by more than half of magazine readers; people care about what the product does for them. H.E. Warren 1935 "How to Understand Why People Buy" cited.
- p.23: Young & Rubicam rule cited: people buy on emotion and justify with logic; force an emotional response by touching a basic want or need.
- p.25-26: Mental-movie ladder example: "Go somewhere and do something" is rewritten in 7 steps (get something, go to the kitchen, get food, open the oven, pull out the pizza, then a full sensory pizza paragraph). Rule: each step adds a specific place, object or action verb; action words create moving pictures.
- p.27: Five-step chapter summary: (1) people have the LF8 wants, (2) strongest appeals use them, (3) write copy that makes prospects visually demonstrate the product in their heads, (4) then make them believe it delivers (social proof, guarantees), (5) then push them to act (battle inertia).
- p.29-30: Fear-copy examples with numbers: one bacteria cell becomes more than 8 million in under 24 hours (disinfectant spray kills 99.9% of germs, about $5 a can); 10% of the weight of a two-year-old pillow is dead dust mites and their feces (anti-mite mattress cover $60, pillow cases under $10); groomer's noose example for a dog groomer.
- p.30: Fear causes stress; three stress types named: stress of loss (missing a big sale), concern for personal safety (choosing tires), future regret (not buying side-curtain airbags).
- p.32-33: Purell case: introduced to food service/healthcare 1988, consumer market 1997. Supporting stats used in its marketing: 2003 ASM survey, more than 30% of people in New York airport restrooms, 19% in Miami and 27% at Chicago O'Hare do not wash hands; Wirthlin 2003 phone survey: only 58% wash after sneezing/coughing, 77% after changing a diaper; JAMA 1972: fecal bacteria and Staph aureus on 13% of 200 coins and 42% of notes.
- p.33: Listerine created the "halitosis" fear in 1900 and reached a 53% market share; deodorant Odorno's 1919 Ladies Home Journal headline about the curve of a woman's arm raised sales 112% even though 200 women cancelled subscriptions in disgust; lesson: make the feared problem an issue, then make the fix easy (travel bottle on key chain).
- p.33: Deadlines and scarcity (limited offer, one-day sale, while supplies last) are a form of fear and tap secondary want #9 (bargains).
- p.35-36: Ego morphing: luxury ads use almost no persuasive copy, only images. Perfume/cologne ads show models not even wearing the product; 2008 cologne sales expected $1.6 billion, women's fragrances $3.2 billion. Stec and Bernstein Balance Theory 1999: people with a desired trait buy to publicise it, people without it buy to appear to have it.
- p.36-37: Ego-appeal tactics by business type: karate school names the MMA stars who train there and sells "I train with [star]" T-shirts; expensive dog groomer shows celebrity clients' dogs; stationery printer offers a free job (250 letterheads and envelopes) to local celebrities (newscasters, political leaders) and then names them; security firm headline pattern "Why do more local bank presidents / jewelry store owners choose [firm] to protect their own homes?"
- p.38-39: Transfer cases: a Fraternal Order of Police mailer moves voters on a crime bill; Proactiv used Jessica Simpson; Quaker Oats (logo since 1877) launched Wilford Brimley campaign in 1987, which reversed a 5-year sales decline despite the 1990 NEJM Sachs study disputing oat-bran cholesterol claims.
- p.39: Good Housekeeping Seal (since 1909) is only given to products whose ads are accepted in the magazine; publisher promises refund or replacement of defective products within two years.
- p.39-40: Hypnotist jumbo postcard: adding the logo of the National Guild of Hypnotists raised credibility although probably not 1 in 100 recipients knew the organisation. Process: prospect sees a credibility symbol, then questions less of the sales argument.
- p.41: Maslow: need to belong ranks third after physiological and safety needs.
- p.42: Aspirational-group example: if the target market's median age is 34, do not show a 70-year-old cyclist, and do not show an unknown customer (even one spending $1,000 a month); show a recognised star cyclist.
- p.43: Gap (opened San Francisco 1969, name suggests "generation gap"), 3,100 stores and 2007 revenue over $15.8 billion: associative plus dissociative strategy. Bandwagon slogan examples: Walgreens "the pharmacy America trusts", Jif "choosy moms", Eucerin "#1 dermatologist-recommended brand for dry skin", British Airways "world's favorite airline".
- p.44-45: Means-End Chain exercise: keep asking "what is the benefit of that?" from a feature (a business sign) until the core end benefit is reached; sell what the product does for the buyer tomorrow, not today.
- p.47-48: Inoculation 3 steps: (1) warn of an impending attack, (2) make a weak attack, (3) encourage a strong defense. Examples: auto body shop warns that competitors quote over $1,000 for a small dent and $800 for a windshield chip; pizza "delivers in 28 minutes or it's free" style claims create critical consumers predisposed to you.
- p.51-52: Belief re-ranking "Kung Fu concession" example (non-violent person accepts self-defense training after crime surge); a 100% response rate is unrealistic, but many will amend beliefs to fit new perceptions.
- p.54-55: ELM example: a $928,000 house is bought with central (left-brain) reasoning (5-year ARM vs 30-year fixed, 18% debt-to-income, 20% down avoids PMI), a can of beans with peripheral cues; still give basic data to peripheral thinkers.
- p.56: Cialdini spent three years undercover in sales jobs for his book on influence.
- p.59-61: Reciprocation: a free 2-cent pen or a $1 bill enclosed with a 4-page, 100-question survey raises compliance; Moving Target used reciprocation as primary strategy for more than 16 years (new-neighbor gift certificates). Free-gift ideas by business: consultant 30 minutes free, copy shop 50 free photocopies, cigar shop free cigar.
- p.61-62: Commitment/consistency: ad that asks four questions, each answer leading to the next, until the prospect is committed; petition-then-$3-donation example.
- p.63: Scarcity case: 1983 Cabbage Patch Kids craze; a crowd of 1,000 stormed a Zayre store in Wilkes-Barre, Pennsylvania, after waiting eight hours.
- p.64-65: Examples vs statistics demo: Rolls-Royce Phantom ($403,000), emotional example (453 HP) vs spec list (6,749 cc V12, 338 kW, 531 ft lb). Research: well-written examples relate to personal experience and need less mental effort (Petty & Cacioppo 1986).
- p.68: Two-sided message example (fly swatter maker: popular in the 1940s-50s, now it's the 21st century) and "takeaway" technique (telling readers the product is not for them to increase desire).
- p.69: Myth "people don't see your ad until it runs seven times" addressed; the 3/5 exposure limit does not mean you should not run an ad more than three times (frequency vs fatigue).
- p.70-71: Rhetorical question examples (Dial, Rinso 1926, Rolaids). Research conflict: some studies say it helps, Gayle, Preiss and Allen 1998 meta-analysis found "no appreciable influence on persuasion", Swasy and Munch 1985 found it can make readers feel pressured and see the writer as less authoritative.
- p.72-73: Evidence demo: offering a sealed envelope for a $20 bill gets no takers until people know what's inside (WIIFM, "What's in it for me"); offering a visible $20 bill for $1 gets dozens. Reinhard 1988 definition of evidence.
- p.75-77: Length-implies-strength cases: "101 Success Stories" 4-page brochure with 101 testimonials and photos; 8.5 x 14 inch "Speak Out!" testimonial flyer; Day-Timers ad with 22 reasons to buy; politician's "Torpy's Tricky 200" 50-page document of 200 flip-flops: not 1 in 1,000 readers fact-check.
#### Chapter 3: 41 techniques
- p.79: Edison: 1,093 patents, tested more than 3,000 theories and 6,000 plant materials, carbonized bamboo filament gave 1,200+ hours; lesson: reuse proven methods.
- p.81-82: Flesch formula steps: count words (contractions, hyphenated words, abbreviations, figures, symbols each count as one word), count syllables as pronounced, count sentences (units separated by period, colon, semicolon, question mark, exclamation point or dash), average syllables per word, average words per sentence. Grade table 0-30 college grad, 30-50 college, 50-60 10th-12th, 60-70 8th-9th, 70-80 7th, 80-90 6th, 90-100 5th.
- p.83-84: Test-paragraph comparison: plain money-making paragraph scored Flesch 72.1, grade 6.4 (13.1 words/sentence, 4.1 chars/word); formal version scored 34.1, grade 14.7 (25.3 words/sentence). US 2007: only about 30% of 25-29-year-olds had a bachelor's degree; about 3.4 million 16-24-year-olds were dropouts.
- p.85: Definite-word rewrites: "become financially successful" becomes a specific weekly dollar figure ("up to $2,495 every week").
- p.86-87: Personal pronouns: load copy with you, me, I, he, she, they; repeating "you learned" increases perceived volume (length-implies-strength).
- p.88-89: Feature-Benefit Exercise (workshop): seller states a feature, prospect answers "Big deal, what's in it for me?" until a benefit is given (e.g. 500-sheet tray, Ultra-Draft mode using 50% less ink). Do not advertise new equipment or a 10th anniversary unless it benefits the buyer.
- p.91-92: People see 247 (Consumer Reports) to 3,000+ (Newspaper Association of America) ads a day; 60% of ad readers read only the headline (Ogilvy: unless the headline sells, 90% of the money is wasted). Headline rewrite examples: food server workshop promising to raise tips by 512% or money back; bakery offering an 8.5-pound fudge pie free.
- p.94-95: Miller 1956 "magical number seven, plus or minus two"; people grasp 5 to 6 words in one glance. Saturday Evening Post study of 2,500 ads (1939-40): headline readership by length: up to 3 words 87.3%, 4-6 86.3%, 7-9 84%, 10-12 82.5%, 13+ 77.9%; shortest beats longest by about 1/7; biggest drop above 12 words. Rule: (1) never drop the biggest benefit, (2) keep it short where possible.
- p.97: Deadline phrase list: call before [date], supplies strictly limited, offer expires [date], price guaranteed only until [date], good only before 4 p.m., seating limited to 50, no rain checks, first 50 callers. A missing deadline signals the offer is always available.
- p.98-100: 22 headline starters: Free, New, At Last, This, Announcing, Warning, Just Released, Now, Here's, These, Which of, Finally, Look, Presenting, Introducing, How, Amazing, Do You, Would You, Can You, If You, Starting Today (example: dance 97% better). Headline must (1) grab attention and (2) motivate reading; self-interest headlines promise a personal benefit.
- p.101-103: 12 ways to lure readers into body copy (same headline): empathise with the problem, ask a question, name the expert/source, give an instruction ("raise your left hand..."), challenge ("read pages 8 and 9, no more"), editorial aside, testimonial, play reporter (dateline news style, e.g. seven-year study), tease, quote, super-detailed specs (8.5 x 11 inch hardcover, 327 pages, 10 chapters, 45 tools), very short first sentence ("Don't you hate it?").
- p.104: Set the ad in a circular border instead of a rectangle to stand out on a page of rectangles (one in ten different gets the attention).
- p.105-106: Reverse type (white on black) pitfall: Holmes 1931, black-on-white readable at about 66 inches vs reversed about 55 inches; another test: reversed 4% less legible when compared side by side, 16% less legible when shown alone. Only reverse if the ad is crammed among other ads.
- p.107-109: Extreme specificity: teacher called about 25 pizza shops asking "what makes your pizza better" for a group of 10; none gave specifics. Rewrite with specifics (renovated, nearly doubled dining room, 80 years of baking, 100% cold-pressed virgin olive oil). Hardware store example: 343 kinds of fasteners, 28 types of nails, 86 wire gauges, 43 sandpaper grits, 16 hammer styles, 28 screwdriver kinds, 47 key types, 354,000 bolts and screws in daily inventory, money-back guarantee. Ask: what is obvious to me but unknown to my market?
- p.110: Ogilvy layout (Two-Thirds/One-Third): top two thirds one big photo, headline directly under it, copy below starting with a drop cap, logo lower right; drop initial raises readership by an average of 13% (Ogilvy). Reverse Ogilvy: one-third photo, headline, two-thirds copy. Always put captions under pictures (Caples).
- p.111-113: Typefaces: Wheildon 1986: 12% comprehended sans-serif body text vs 67% for serif; Garamond comprehended by 66% of subjects, Times Roman less than half of that, Helvetica 12.5%. British Medical Council 1926: sans serif causes "irradiation". Paterson and Tinker 1932: Cloister Black slows reading 16.5%, American Typewriter 5.1%. Recommended print serifs: Adobe Caslon, Adobe Garamond, ITC Stone Serif, Janson Text 55 Roman. Top 10 newspaper typefaces (Ascender): Poynter Series, Franklin Gothic, Helvetica, Utopia, Times, Nimrod, Century Old Style, Interstate, Bureau Grotesque, Miller.
- p.113-114: All-caps text slows reading 11.8% (Paterson and Tinker 1956); initial-cap headlines read 18.9% better than all caps in another test; Tinker 1946 blink-rate test; word shape test (caps form rectangles).
- p.114-115: Online type: print is 300 dpi, Mac screens 72 dpi, Windows 96 dpi. Fidelity study (Tullis, Boynton, Hersh 1995): Arial and MS Sans Serif at 9.75 pt most preferred. Bernard and Mills 2000: readers preferred 12 pt over 10 pt. Bernard et al. 2001: Arial and Times New Roman read faster than Courier, Schoolbook, Georgia; 12 pt faster than 10 pt. SURL 2002: most legible Arial, Courier, Verdana; at 10 pt Verdana preferred, at 12 pt Arial preferred, Times New Roman least. Rule: online use Arial for 12 pt and larger, Verdana below 12 pt, rarely below 10 pt, 14 pt for older readers; avoid reversed text.
- p.116-117: Use a professional designer (owning a hammer does not make a carpenter). Questions in copy act as pop quizzes that keep readers alert.
