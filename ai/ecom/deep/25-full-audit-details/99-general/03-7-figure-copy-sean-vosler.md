## General (lessons whose topics have no chapter) (page 3 of 7)

### 7 Figure Copy - Sean Vosler (Dokument) (Course C) [L0196, 2020+] (continued)

- Value-battle case, Apple vs Microsoft: Apple's products were mostly 2nd or 3rd generation technologies (mouse, touch screen, mp3 player, PC, iPhone) but Apple popularised them; core values "best in the market" and "best demonstration of product benefits" let Apple charge 2-3-5x what similarly featured competitors charged. The "Get a Mac" ("I'm a Mac / I'm a PC") campaign ran 2006-2009 with over 66 ads, defining and attacking an enemy (the PC) and the values of each user type. Small brands can do the same by standing for one idea the audience believes in (e.g. Apple's "Think Different"; the author's enemy "lack of knowledge of what truly drives sales", value "learning matters", tagline "never stop learning"). Exercise: choose the three values that best define your product/brand from a list (accountability, beauty, boldness, ... warmth, wisdom; full list "The Ultimate List of Core Values"). (doc p.183-185)
- Boogie-man enemy sentence structure: "The [enemy] [problem with] [common belief] of X is not Y, it's Z." Example: "Your product isn't 'not selling' because of its quality, or even your lack of experience. It's not selling because your messaging isn't connecting with your audience." Raise tension from the local tension (immediate consequence, e.g. lack of sales from poor messaging) to the universal tension (the big-picture issue, e.g. what really drives success), following Ronald Tobias, "Master Plots" ("without tension, there is no plot"). (doc p.186-187)
- Combined enemy example: "Do you have nasty bed bugs? {struggle} We hate {value} those... monsters as much as you do! The problem with most sprays on the market today isn't just that they aren't as effective as they used to be {boogie-man, local}, it's that they can be astonishingly unhealthy {boogie-man, universal}! That's why we created Bed Bug Bomb. The fastest {value} way to get rid of bed bugs when nothing else seems to work {value}, period! {solution}" Rule: the marketing defines the enemies, the brand understands them, the product vanquishes them; keep the monsters real, no fear-mongering. (doc p.188)
- Exercise for studying others' marketing: save persuasive content, highlight where the writer "fans the flames", match persuasive parts to the 5 foundations, and record structures in a copy research journal (example studied: a highly engaged Alex Becker Facebook post). Edit an active sales page, lead page or email to add more of the reader's dreams and goals at the expense of self-promotion; if stuck, interview a successful buyer about their fears, desires and "the before times". (doc p.192)
- Contrarian Copy Structure (author claims it generated millions in sales; used as the starting outline for nearly all his pieces): 1. ATTENTION: hook; position the problem and why it matters; allegory/metaphor so readers feel understood. 2. ACKNOWLEDGEMENT: what most people think (the misconception). 3. AGGRAVATE: here is the truth (the claim). 4. AUTHORITY: why it is true (the proof): scientific research, logical examples, emotional examples. 5. ANGST: the damage the common belief does (the stakes): what it has done in the reader's life, what continued belief leads to, a list of the "problem of the problem". 6. AMBIGUITY: what others or the author did to fix it and what you can do (clarity): what to do now, the specific steps, beliefs changed, how to remind yourself in hard times to maintain change. 7. APPEAL: summary of benefits and call to action. End goal: build trust by gifting the reader an epiphany (acknowledge their current perspective, introduce a new, deeper one), turning a buyer into a believer. (doc p.196-197)
- Where to use the Contrarian Copy Structure: sales pages (every product or service needs a full persuasive sales page that educates and pre-qualifies leads, not a feature list plus contact form), email campaigns (author wrote close to 1,000 emails with it and attributes a trackable $40M in sales, about $40,000 revenue per email written), webinars ("a sales page with interaction"), content marketing, cornerstone content (a few in-depth useful pieces beat 100 mediocre posts), long social media posts, and ads (long-form YouTube and Facebook ads that follow it convert). Not needed for pure brand-awareness billboard campaigns. Benefits of starting from a structure ("tracing"): beat writer's block, build on proven techniques, avoid guesswork on structure, later add your own innovations. (doc p.194-195)
- Belief disharmony (cognitive dissonance) in copy: introducing a new idea that conflicts with an existing belief creates discomfort the reader must resolve; your job is to lead them to resolve it, presenting facts empathetically and reducing the pain of change. Proving your point without a change in behaviour is "winning the battle but losing the war". The author credits copy built on this with well over 8 figures in sales. (doc p.200-201)
- Two conditions are required for real dissonance: 1. the reader must feel they have a choice/control over the matter (no effect on factual beliefs they cannot change, or on buyers contractually bound to a competitor or without purchase authority); with "layered misplaced beliefs" ("I'm not the type of person who does that") first convince them they are that kind of person; 2. there must be clear, meaningful consequences (stakes) they care about. (doc p.201)
- Example contrarian claims: email subject "can going to college ruin your life?" (belief: college is necessary for a good life); "Most people assume you have to 'hustle' 100s or 1000s of hours before your first five or six-figure month... with the right system it can happen faster" ; "there's really only one metric that will determine if [a product] is going to be successful: does it bring enough value to the end-user?"; subject "secret behind 20m in revenue by 27 + (one important shift in perspective)"; native ad -> blog post challenging "You can't time the market". The challenge often opens the piece as the attention grabber. Balance claim intensity: too mild lacks stakes, too "hot" gets dismissed as crazy. (doc p.202)
- Three ways readers resolve dissonance: 1. accept (A. change behaviour, or B. agree but not change); 2. dispute your idea (if they can without being factually wrong, revisit where your logic or emotion breaks); 3. pull in outside ideas to rationalise theirs (pre-empt by acknowledging and refuting alternatives). If readers agree but do not buy ("you're right, but I don't care"), fix: 1. check for lack of empathy (e.g. an anti-vaping product that never acknowledges how hard cravings are and that many failed to quit); 2. make the stakes clear (e.g. that vaping causes cancer and shortens life); 3. accept that some deeply entrenched minds are not worth the cost to change ("you're not going to host an intervention to make a sale"). (doc p.203)
- Contrarian Copy steps in detail: Step 1 acknowledge a false belief or misconception commonly held in the reader's community (point of contention, e.g. "many people think XYZ is complicated, however it's actually very simple", or a seemingly extreme stance you then contextualise); Step 2 introduce a counter "contagion" claim that cannot logically coexist with their current understanding (rule: "The goal isn't only to be right, it's to change a belief"; if the reader already agrees you are preaching to the choir and the CTA becomes about improving their current mission); Step 3 prove the claim with an effective argument, then turn the new idea into a eureka moment. Worked example: the author's sales page claims copywriting is the #1 entrepreneurial skill and its title makes readers ask "what is the most valuable skill?". (doc p.206-212)
- Contrarian Message Diagnostic Toolkit (where a reader is lost): does the claim conflict with their beliefs? (no = they happily agree); does it matter enough to them? (no = sale lost; raise the stakes); does the argument make logical sense? (no = give more reasonable proof); do they feel control over the conditions? (no = build a sub-argument addressing the underlying "no control/choice" false belief); do they have "ah ha, got you!" outside information that counters it? (yes = find and address realistic outside arguments). Passing all checks = new belief internalised and trust built. Novel ideas trigger dopamine (study: absolute coding of stimulus novelty in the human substantia nigra/VTA), which aids learning, memory, motivation and bonding; the reader's mindset decides whether the novelty feels positive. (doc p.211)
- Don Draper "Carousel" pitch (Mad Men S1E13, Kodak slide projector) mapped to the structure: Acknowledgement = client belief that technology/new matters most; Attention = curiosity hook with a strong claim; Aggravate = technology is only a "flash", nostalgia is more powerful; Authority = story of mentor copywriter Teddy; Angst = nostalgia beats even "new"; Ambiguity = "It's not a space ship, it's a time machine"; Appeal. Lesson: evidence can be emotional, not only factual, and lift the message from a local problem (technology) to a universal one (connection with the past). (doc p.208)
- Contrarian structure results: the author's sales page built on the 7 steps (provocative metaphor + strong claim; what most people think are the key skills; stakes; authority quote; angst contrasting knowing vs not knowing the skill; simple solution and shortcut; CTA) converted Facebook traffic at 9.4%. Email example ("can going to college ruin your life?") uses a magazine cover quote of a woman with $152,000 in college debt as authority; in a follow-up sequence, a quick CTA near the top flags that the promotion ends in a few hours. (doc p.214-215)
- Hero's Journey for copy (Joseph Campbell, The Hero With A Thousand Faces; adapted by Jonah Sachs, Story Wars; Christopher Vogler, The Writer's Journey): the customer is the hero, you are the mentor/guide; different audience segments are at different phases of the 12-phase journey, so adjust copy to their phase. Depth of copy depends on product complexity and price (a $12 coffee thermos needs far less than a $20,000 coaching program). Do not tell readers they have superpowers; acknowledge they have the abilities and just need the right methods. Not every piece needs all elements; weave hero, product and brand attributes into pieces rather than literally writing a story each time. (doc p.217-220)
- Change is scary: avoid asking for too much change early in the marketing process; it is often better to present an entirely new journey than to force change (e.g. adding something to a diet instead of changing the diet). Readers act when an inner calling for advancement collides with an external opportunity for relief; they often refuse the first call, and if they class your CTA as a fantasy not fit for them, re-examine your core argument. Hope builds more lasting connection than fear. Benefit categories of values: relationships, peace of mind, legacy/influence, time, attention. (doc p.219-222)
- Dove case (David Aaker): Dove, launched as a bar soap in 1955, did about $200 million in sales in the early 1990s and over $3 billion in 2011 (last reported year); a key driver was the "Campaign for Real Beauty", originated in Brazil by Ogilvy & Mather in 2004, connecting the brand to women's self-esteem. For each journey phase consider: how it helps understand the audience internally and externally; your role; likely emotions (encourage positive, mitigate negative); which marketing pieces to create and where in the funnel. (doc p.223-224)
- Phase 1 "Ordinary World": define the audience's accepted world vs dream world (most people do not give up on dreams, they put them off). Examples of false beliefs: Sally wants clearer skin but blames genetics; Tom wants the right girl but is "too shy"; a New Yorker lives paycheck to paycheck and thinks "the cards are stacked against him"; Jessica thinks she "just doesn't have what it takes to diet and exercise". Doctor/coffee example: acknowledge the fear, show 30 others who succeeded and now enjoy the result, open a loop to how life improves; make it about the results, not the seller's talent. Phase 1 goals: 1. make them understand where they are and where they want to be; 2. wake them up to possibilities to reach that dream world or a better one. (doc p.227-230)
- Ordinary-World research questions (worksheet link.sean.co/hj-1), asked in relation to your product/niche: 1. What dream worlds could the hero harbour (better health, wealth, happiness; giving back to community, family, environment; more time with loved ones, and how much)? 2. Why are they putting off the pursuit (waiting for "the right time"; not enough resources; waiting for an event such as an age, a bank balance or the right mindset; fear of others' opinions; not knowing what is possible for "normal people")? 3. What emotional fears hold them back (too shy or outgoing; not enough patience, knowledge or experience; past pain of overconfidence and failure; being told all their life they lack what it takes)? 4. What deep hopes do they hold unconsciously or would you wish for them? 5. What past positive experiences have they had with your industry/product? If you do not believe in your product, refine it or find something better to sell. (doc p.230-231)
- "Establishing shot" headlines use time-based language (Now, Tonight, Instantly) to imply a dream world is waiting: "Your Best Summer Body", "Amazing Sex Tonight", "Look Better INSTANTLY", "Big Arms Now!". Ordinary-to-dream-world framing is especially useful at the first touch of a campaign (content marketing or ads); ad examples: "What if you could travel several times a year to STUNNING locations...?", "Sell More. Work Less.", "You've worked hard to live well.", "Is your business 'stuck'? We've developed a specific formula...". The dream can be an attitude, not a material thing (Hornitos tequila "A Shot Worth Taking" campaign funded real people's crowdfunded "shots" in life). (doc p.232-233)
- Character Sketch exercise (under 30 focused minutes): answer the Ordinary-World questions, guessing with empathy where unsure, and talk to current customers or trusted people for feedback. Then a "Day in the Life" exercise of 2-3 paragraphs covering only daily-life elements your product affects: 1. the hero's life before your product; 2. after; 3. the (hypothetical) buying experience. (doc p.234)
- Phase 2 "Call to Adventure": marketing first joins the conversation already in the reader's mind by "putting truths in front of the hero" (e.g. "you need to lose weight for your health"); the harder the truth is to accept, the stronger the bond once accepted. Two truth categories: 1. what the dream is and why they CAN reach it; 2. what has actually held them back (internal and external): the villain. (doc p.235)
- Teach, Transform, Transact: learning removes fear, teaching earns trust. Emotional events form strong, lasting neural connections (neuroplasticity, Eugene Rubin MD); since you cannot desensitise readers in a simulation, educate them to "unlearn" defective beliefs. (doc p.243)
- Bloom's Taxonomy (1950s, six levels: knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, evaluation) applied to copy: PLAN where your target audience sits (low understanding = more context; knowledgeable = cover basics quickly); EVALUATE whether the piece ascended the reader through each level. Example sales-page sections for a copywriting book aimed at business owners at the knowledge stage: knowledge (what copywriting is and why wealth correlates with it), comprehension (different methods and schools of thought), application (an action strategy usable now), synthesis (successful vs failed copy), then analysis and evaluation. (doc p.249-252)
- Editing rule "remove, reduce, and don't recycle" (Copywriting Quickie #7): say it once and say it right; if a point needs expansion for some readers, say so, so advanced readers can skip ahead. Procedure: 1. write normally; 2. step away for at least 24 hours; 3. revise to cut 20% of the words and 10% of the sentences ("killing your darlings", Stephen King). (doc p.253)
- Research definition: distilling data into knowledge, knowledge into principles, and principles into actionable wisdom; if the audience knows more than you, you lose your leverage. Research your product, its industry and your audience; keep research notebooks (author uses Evernote) and follow a set system to avoid endless re-reading, disinformation and missing information (gold-ring metaphor: mine ore, separate metals, smelt, refine to bars, craft the ring). (doc p.270-275)
- Reverse Product Development: use copy principles (benefit of the benefit, raising the stakes, the customer's journey) to write and rewrite the marketing messaging for a hypothetical product BEFORE building it; this shows which features to add and who the product is for. Rule of thumb: "if no one's selling it yet either you have a billion-dollar idea OR your idea might not be as good as you think." Great marketing on a bad product is "adding saffron to a turd". Most profitable ideas come from a deliberate search for solutions to real problems, not a flash of inspiration. (doc p.297-298)
- Information products: recommended for any business (high margins, low front-end investment, enhanced credibility, new funnel entry points, confidence). Market data cited: Lynda.com sold to LinkedIn for $1.5 billion; MasterClass raised $80 million; Amazon roughly $150M per year in Kindle sales; range from a few-dollar Kindle book to $50,000 masterminds. Think in monthly income: about 80 sales per week can mean $20,000+ per month with the right strategy, which is more attainable than the "$1M = 10,000 sales at $100" math; gaining 1,000+ customers immediately or building a $10,000 program for 100 buyers is unrealistic for beginners. The bar for "quality" has never been higher; the opportunity is giving structure to information overload ("putting ideas together in novel ways", Kaye Ramos). (doc p.298-305)
- Strategy beats advantages (dung beetle Onthophagus taurus: strongest animal at 1,141x its body weight, yet hornless males win mates by digging side tunnels). Match the strategy to your resources: little money -> rely on time and experience; little time -> invest in leverage (employees, software); neither -> a creative unique strategy. Failed big-company products cited as poor strategy despite advantages: Facebook Phone, Amazon Phone, Blackberry, Google+, Google Glass, Microsoft Zune. Persistence is itself a strategy. (doc p.301-304)
- Who should build an info product (5 traits): invests time and energy to learn and create; humble enough to admit needing help and determined to find it; listens to audience needs instead of assuming; enjoys self-improvement and helping others improve; understands fear comes from lack of understanding. Expertise helps but is not required (the strategy builds experience); enjoyment is derived from doing, not the other way round. (doc p.307-309)
- Value Ascension Strategy (info products), base goal $20,000 per month revenue (enough to reinvest and live on after tax): Product #1, a "Step by Step Tactical Guide" solving one specific problem, as a book or mini-course, priced $24 to $49; Product #2, a "Time Framed Strategic Guide" as a step-by-step video course, priced $199 to $499. Objective: a sustainable info product solving specific problems; goal: $20k/mo in sales in 30 days; measure: at least 80 sales per month of the core product with a 20% take rate on the upsell product. Context: over 500,000 new businesses are started every month in the US. Three milestones: 1. compass = unflinching commitment to quality (without perfectionism); 2. map = working list of assets to create, quality standards and faster methods (start from a working strategy and adapt, faster than experimenting); 3. vessel = products, funnel, credibility, strategic partners, voice/brand, distribution. Author claims clients reach upper six to seven figures per month with this strategy, some with only one core product line. (doc p.310-316)
- AIDA variants listed: AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action); AIDCA (adds Conviction: testimonials, endorsements, statistics, research, demo, guarantees); IDCA for landing pages when the ad already captured attention; Lavidge's Hierarchy of Effects (Awareness, Knowledge, Liking, Preference, Conviction, Purchase); McGuire (Presentation, Attention, Comprehension, Yielding, Retention, Behavior); Modified AIDA (Awareness, Interest, Conviction, Desire, Action); AIDAS (adds Satisfaction); AISDALSLove (Awareness, Interest, Search, Desire, Action, Like/dislike, Share, Love/hate); CAB (Cognition, Affect, Behavior). (doc appendix p.1)
- Danny Iny 6+1 formula: 1. consider the context; 2. grab attention; 3. move the reader swiftly to desire a solution; 4. present the consequence of failing to act ("the gap"); 5. present the solution, telling only as much as needed to reach the next step; 6. call the prospect to act. (doc appendix p.1)
- PAS (Dan Kennedy, "the most reliable sales formula ever invented"): Problem, Agitation, Solution; Sean Mitchell variant adds Discredit (discredit other solutions) before Solution. Henry Hoke Sr. 4 Ps: Picture, Promise, Prove, Push; second 4P variant: Problem, Promise, Proof, Proposal. ACCA: raise Awareness, increase Comprehension of the problem, inspire Conviction, rally to Action. AAPPA: Attention, Advantages, Proof, Persuade, Action. PAPA: Problem, Advantages of solving it, Proof you can solve it, Action. QUEST: Qualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, Transition. SLAP (shorter pages, inexpensive items): Stop, Look, Act, Purchase. AIDPPC (Robert Collier, sales letters): Attention, Interest, Description, Persuasion (loss aversion, testimonials, future pacing), Proof (demos, testimonials, endorsements), Close. (doc appendix p.1-2)
- Jack Lacey 5-Point formula (lead-gen/landing pages, one-page sites): 1. What will you do for me if I listen? 2. How will you do it? 3. Who is responsible for the promises? 4. Who have you done this for? 5. What will it cost me? AICPBSAWN: Attention (biggest benefit/problem, USP), Interest, Credibility, Proof, Benefits (list all, bullets), Scarcity, Action, Warn (what happens if they do not act), Now. (doc appendix p.2, p.12)
