## General (lessons whose topics have no chapter) (page 6 of 7)

### ca hvertising (Dokument) (Course C) [L0340, undated] (continued)

- p.118-123: "Granny Rule" of direct mail (meet prospects in their world, like a salesman in person): list all benefits, rank by importance to the customer, put #1 in the letter opening; put ease and speed in the first sentence (examples: 22% better gas mileage, 20 pounds of lean muscle, 5-minute memory trick or double money back, luxury real estate at 50% off, double/triple reading speed in 3 days). People expect 99% of their mail to be trash, so each component's first job is attention. Handwritten-style note on the envelope after an info request ("[Name]: Here's the information we talked about", "Please read this letter before 9:30 tonight", then justify the deadline in the letter). Do not open like a job application.
- p.124-125: Mailing-list selection criteria include recency, frequency and high dollar amount spent (shows more commitment than someone who spent $2.50). Mark reply envelopes with active wording such as "Order enclosed" or "24-hour processing".
- p.125-126: First testimonial used in 1926 by Ponds cold cream. Testimonial permission form: questions plus a disclaimer granting use of quotes in full or edited form, with or without name and city/state (no address/phone), compensation 10 copies of the first printed piece; or enclose $1.00 as consideration.
- p.127: Guillotine principle: crop a dramatic face (e.g. screaming 235-pound mugger for a tear-gas ad). Tip: put your own photo at the top and make the headline a quote from you; quotes in headlines can boost reading 25% (checklist).
- p.128-130: PVAs (positive visual adjectives) rewrites: "rake in $2,750 cash weekly"; cleaning service (hospital-bright walls and floors, sanitized restrooms, gleaming windows, fluffy deodorized carpets); oranges hand-picked by a 73-year-old mother. Businesses that skip PVAs lose to those that use them.
- p.131-136: Mental movies with VAKOG (visual, auditory, kinesthetic, olfactory, gustatory): every experience is a blend of these five; build internal representations. Exterminator example: a female black widow lays 4 to 9 egg sacs per summer, each with up to 750 eggs hatching in 14 to 30 days, turning a DIY job into an $89 call. Pool cleaner example: $250, two-year warranty, $50 rebate, more than 1,250,000 sold, assembles in under 10 minutes, saves $850 a year vs a pool service.
- p.137-139: Battling inertia: two steps, (1) make it easy to act, (2) ask for action. Accept all major cards, checks, money orders, PayPal; offer a payment plan ("just $14.99 a month for three months"); say "It's easy to order" (or easy to get a free estimate / schedule an inspection).
- p.139-142: USP: do not be a 5-pound bag of sugar; diner case (Nifty Fifty's) differentiates with an explicit list of what makes it unique. Name upgrades: "The Hardware Superstore", "The Office Cleaning Technicians", "A World of Pets Under One Small Roof", "Denver's #1 Graphic Designer". Free-report positioning. Personal-story headline formulas: "19-year-old college kid discovers...", "Chinese billionaire reveals 14 secrets...", "Idaho farmer says...", physical-trait gimmick (6'5", 275-pound man offers the biggest computer discounts; photo from floor level).
- p.143: "Half-Page Island": two columns wide, three-quarters of a page deep, dominates the page for less than a full page; small ads suit the two-step process (inquire, then send full info).
- p.144-147: Authority positioning steps: (1) regard yourself as an expert with information to share, (2) publish it in as many forms as possible: 3- to 10-page reports in print and PDF (e.g. save up to 27% on 4-color printing, make your resume 325% more attention-getting), a newsletter-style mailer with numbered "booster" tips, a Q&A column with about three questions per column and your headshot.
- p.147-148: Sales letter disguised as a survey: thank customer, ask 1-10 rating questions, then offer a reward for returning it (car detailer example: $10 spray for $5, 50% off, if returned by a date; keeps finish up to 50% longer within 30 days). Works because people like giving opinions and payment slips easily into the same return envelope. Format: personal letter on 8.5 x 11 business stationery.
- p.148-151: Pictures (Roper Starch 1991, 2,000 consumers, 10 US markets, 650 newspaper ads): ads with 50% visuals noted 30% more than no visuals; 75% visuals noted 50% more and "read most" 60% higher; 4-9 visuals +30% noted; 10+ visuals +55% noted and +70% read most; showing the product +13%; product-in-use shots another +13% over product-only; people in pictures +nearly 25% noted. Gallup/Kimberly-Clark (29,000 readers, 20 Sunday papers, 16 cities) picture preference: children/babies, mothers with babies, groups of adults, animals, sports scenes, celebrities, food. Parade: babies, mothers and babies, animals, personalities, food. Always give photos context (caption).
- p.151-153: Grabbers: attach an object to a letter, e.g. a $1 bill stapled on top with copy "I'll show you how to save 100 of these" (carpet), "20 of these" (day spa mailing to women within 5 miles), "10,000 of these in three months" (network marketing). Robert Collier used it in 1937. Test with an A-B split: 500 letters with grabber, 500 without.
- p.154-157: Long copy beats short when it stays interesting (2 hours with a salesperson vs 10 minutes); car ads appeal to what others will think (LF8 social approval). Web: content on a single long page gave users more success than splitting across pages; one site saw about 5% fewer homepage-only exits and about 20% more enrollments after adding content (Doug Talbott).
- p.157-158: Offer testing: "2 visits for the price of 1" / "Buy one get one free" outperforms the equivalent "50% off" in tests. Test free reports as offers (e.g. "14 things you're doing that hurt your back").
- p.159-161: Survey power (used by salespeople since the 1930s): mail a short letter plus survey (one 8.5 x 11 page, both sides if needed) to e.g. everyone within 5 miles of a pizzeria; ask 1-10 scale questions (go out vs make at home, better than frozen, likelihood to return within two weeks, max price for an 18-inch pie). The secret of response is ease.
- p.161-162: Editorial-style ads: Ogilvy said 50% better readership, Caples 80%, Richard Benson 500-600%. Example structure: news headline ("Local hypnotist announces new way to quit smoking in 48 hours"), dateline, story, then a call/report offer. Never sound too enthusiastic in editorial ads.
- p.162-165: Coupon persuader: Candler 1894 free Coca-Cola tickets, then 2 gallons of syrup to fountains that served 128 free glasses; Coca-Cola in every US state and territory by 1895. Coupon Council stats: 86% of US population uses coupons; about $2.7 billion saved last year; average coupon $1.28 in 2007; users save 11.5% on groceries; $350+ billion offered in 2007; usage by age 18-24 71%, 25-34 87%, 35-44 89%, 45-54 85%, 55-64 90%, 65+ 91%; by income under $25k 86%, $25-50k 85%, $50-75k 88%, $75-100k 88%, $100k+ 81%. Setting copy inside a 3-point coupon border with a very light yellow tint lifted response even without a real coupon; works from 1-inch column ads to brochures.
- p.166-167: 7 online response boosters: e-mail frequency preference (Forrester/NFO) 31-35% once a week, 18% two to three days a week, 13% once a month, 12% daily, 10% two to three times a month, 6% less than once a month; response from under 1% (rented lists, weak offer) to over 20% (own list, strong incentive); 68%+ of e-mail marketing in HTML, about 60% of users can receive it, HTML 200% better response than plain text (Jupiter) but some block HTML; average open rate 69% for opt-in B2B e-zines, 60%+ excellent (Opt-In News); animated ads get at least 15% and up to 40% higher click-through than static; cryptic ads/e-mails can raise click-through 18%. All "possibly outdated" (2008 data).
- p.167-168: Multiple ads in the same issue create a 1+1=3 effect (Starch). 10 most effective multi-ad formations: (1) three single-page ads in sequence on right pages, (2) two single-page ads in different sections, right side, (3) double-page spread, (4) single-page ads on the right, (5) single-page left with strip ad right, (6) single-page left, (7) right-page checkerboard (quarter-page ad in each corner), (8) left-page checkerboard, (9) half-page upper right, (10) half-page lower right.
- p.168-170: Guarantees: Whitman offers a full one-year money-back guarantee. Campbell-style soup case: "double your money back" (soup 13 cents per 20 oz can); only 12 women claimed it. Short guarantees (30/60/90 days) keep buyers watching the deadline; long ones (6 months, 1, 5, 10 years, lifetime) build confidence and avoid the "beat the clock" mindset; use a longer guarantee than competitors and ask why competitors guarantee only 90 days. Skeptic scale example: skepticism 7 of 10 vs desire to believe 5 of 10, the guarantee closes the gap.
- p.170-172: Ad size vs attention (quarter page = 100%): W.D. Scott half page 300%, full page 666%; Strong 141%/215%; Hotchkiss 151%/213%; Starch 168%/314%; Adams 178%; averages 187.6% (half) and 352% (full). E.K. Strong 1927: 1,400,000 replies from 907 ads. Rule: to double attention enlarge the ad 400% (quarter to full page); to triple it, 900%. A 4x bigger ad does not give 4x replies.
- p.173-174: Position: a good ad gets noticed anywhere, but 4 positions win (618 cover-position ads vs 10,789 inside single-page 4-color ads): inside front cover +29% noted, opposite table of contents up to +25%, back cover +22%, inside back cover +6%.
- p.174-177: Color preference order: blue, red, green, violet, orange, yellow (21,000 reports; men put orange 5th, women yellow 5th). Infants/children preferences shift; at age 5 red, green, blue roughly equal; child's lens absorbs about 10% of blue light vs 85% in an elderly eye. Starch color-pair ranking (most preferred blue-yellow, most complementary red-green). Color ads increase in-depth reading 60% over black-and-white and 40% over two-color (Starch).
- p.177-179: Pricing: Walmart favors ending 97; odd-even theory (77, 95, 99 suggest value; $9.77 beats $10.00); round prices ($1,000.00, Saks uses 00) suggest quality vs $999.95. Holdershaw, Gendall, Garland 1997: about 60% of advertised retail prices end in 9, 30% in 5, 7% in 0, the other seven digits just over 3%. Schindler and Kibarian 1996 catalog test: prices ending 99 gave 8% more sales and buyers than 00; 88 equalled 00. Rutgers 2000: dress at $49.99 judged lower quality than at $50. Schindler 1984: 98/99 endings suggest the price was not recently raised; Quigley and Notarantonio 1992: 98/99 endings suggest a sale. Endings 49, 50, 90 do not suggest low price; 79, 88, 98 do convey value; 95 weaker than 99.
- p.179-181: Color psychology: pink prison walls reduce violence; perceived weight of boxes by color (higher = heavier): white 3.1, yellow 3.5, green 4.1, blue 4.7, purple 4.8, gray 4.8, red 4.9, black 5.8; Canada Dry sugar-free ginger ale can changed from red to green and white, sales up more than 25%. Berlin and Kay 1969 (21 languages): color words appear in order black/white, red, green or yellow, yellow or green, blue, brown, then gray/purple/pink/orange.
- p.181-182: White-wrap: Poffenberger, a white-wrapped half page scored 176% attention vs a standard half page (100%); standard full page 141%; a white-wrapped quarter page beats a crowded half page (Poffenberger and Strong). Strong: extra white space should not exceed 60% of the ad area; about 20% extra as white space gives the best effect per cost.
- p.182-185: "Cleverectomy" case: a web designer wanted a catchy pun headline; the recommended headline was plain and specific ("Powerful web pages designed in 24 hours by famous marketing expert for $199"): speed, expert credibility, price, specificity, an offer, clarity. 60% who read only headlines are lost with a vague one.
#### Chapter 4 Hot Lists (p.187-192)
- 22 Response Superchargers: forget style, sell; shout "Free information!"; short sentences; short simple words; long copy; boil out fluff; pile on benefits; show the product, action shots best; say you; selling subheads; selling captions under photos; powerful visual adjectives; sell your product, not the competitor's; give the full sell now; always include testimonials; make it ridiculously easy to act; include a response coupon; set a deadline; free gift for quick replies; say "Order now!"; offer free shipping; a "Bill me"/credit option boosts response 50% or more.
- 9 Ways to Convey Value: shout "Sale!"; give a coupon; diminish the price ("less than a cup of coffee a day"); explain why the price is low ("our boss ordered too many"); amortize ("just $1.25 a day"); state what it is worth, not only what it costs; tell what others paid; deadlines for scarcity; psychological pricing.
- 13 Ways to Make Buying Easy: give street, e-mail and web address; phone number; directions and parking; say "It's easy to order"; accept phone, mail, online and fax orders; credit cards; personal checks; toll-free number; a long, strong guarantee longer than the competition's; installment payments for products over $15 ("3 easy payments of $10.99") shown to boost response 15%.
- 11 Ways to Boost Coupon Returns: tell readers in headline/subhead to return the coupon; "Buy 1 Get 1 Free" instead of "50% off"; big "FREE!" at top; say what the coupon brings and repeat it inside the coupon; show it with a small photo; bold coupon border; hard or soft deadline ("first 100 people"); check-off boxes; "Valuable Coupon" at top; enough room for fill-ins; bold arrows pointing to the coupon.
- 46-point "Killer Ad" checklist: headline features the biggest benefit (#1 rule), is larger and bold, makes an offer, is authoritative; set in initial caps, all caps only for about 4-5 word headlines; headline in quotes can boost reading 25%; "you" among the first words of body copy; sequence attention, interest, desire, proof, action; sell one product per ad (delis and furniture stores excepted); serif face (e.g. Schoolbook) for print, sans serif (Arial, Verdana) for web; tell readers what to do in super-simple numbered steps (clip coupon, bring it by date, save 50%); bullet lists for many benefits; business name and phone large; logo always; directions/maps/landmarks; key each ad to track response; professional designer; headline broken at natural word groups; indent paragraphs; minimal number of elements; relevant art only; one or two typefaces, three max; a picture of a person looking at the reader is one of the strongest attention grabbers.
- p.195-198: Recommended reading list: Hopkins (My Life in Advertising, Scientific Advertising), Caples (Advertising Ideas, Tested Advertising Methods, Making Ads Pay), Ogilvy on Advertising, Robert Collier Letter Book, Schwab (How to Write a Good Advertisement), Printers Ink Small-Space Advertising, Schwartz (Breakthrough Advertising), Watkins (100 Greatest Advertisements), Bayan (Words That Sell, 6,000+ words; More Words That Sell, 3,500), Ries and Trout (Positioning), Bly (Copywriter's Handbook), Baker (Systematic Approach to Advertising Creativity), von Oech (A Whack on the Side of the Head, A Kick in the Seat of the Pants).

### Confessions of a Persuasion Hitman (Dokument) (Course C) [L0378, 2019+]

- [Rule 5, p.65-70] Two-step emotional close after the offer: (1) aspiration with the word "Imagine" (examples: "Imagine waking up six months from now..." / 30 days from now, lost 13 pounds); (2) ONLY if they have not bought: "pain picture" of the cost of doing nothing (example: card debt $10,000 to $15,000), softened by "I'm not saying this to be harsh..."; rule "if you've already made the sale, STOP selling"; robot vacuum example priced $297.
- [Conclusion p.164] Six steps before writing the core sales message: identify the feelings prospects feel now; the feelings they want; stories that tap them; build a bridge between the two; make them feel as many different emotions as possible; build tension and desire, then let them release it by buying. 8 core emotions listed: pain, fear, guilt, shame, anger, joy, love, passion.
- [Rule 4, p.51-52] Dan Kennedy quote "Charge as high a price as you can while keeping a straight face"; quote prices by email/phone; Executive coach's grandmother rule "whatever you want to charge, triple it": she charged $80,000 for a home interior design and about $20,000 just to step in and think about it. Own example: asked friend $20k vs $25k upfront, charged $25k, client said yes.
- [Rule 4 mini-lesson, p.54] Low-price counterexample numbers: Welcome Email template sold for $7, developed over 5 years with millions in ad spend tested; some $7 buyers later attended the $10,000 email copy and deliverability workshop.
- [Rule 3, p.42-48] Income numbers: tennis stringing $25 vs $12-15; tennis coaching $20/h in high school, $70-100/h after college while older pros (35-45) still earn about the same, some coaches charge $40/h after 40 years; hand-copied a sales letter 1 hour per day, one month later got a job writing for a list of over 1 million at $20/h; saved all copy income, quit tennis after 6 months at $3,500-5,000/month; the same skill is now worth $2,000/h for hourly consulting.
- [Rule 6, p.98-99] Objection-as-selling-point example: Special Forces boot ad "Proudly NOT Waterproof" (feet need to breathe, waterproof boots trap sweat), the objection turned into the reason to buy.
- [Rule 6 conclusion, p.165-166] Math-close example for a high-ticket offer: "So you pay $10,000 once and add $25,000 a month to your bottom line. Is that worth it?" then "$25,000 a month is $300,000 a year in found money; if someone said give me $10,000 today and I'll give you $300,000 at the end of the year, what would you say?" Prospects almost always say yes.
- [Rule 9, p.116-118, 124] Copy from calls: first sales letter (dating niche, age 20) written only from phone calls with verbatim answers; skin care letter: asked his mum why she uses skin care, answer "I just want to look in the mirror and feel pretty again" became the first sentence; people say "it's exactly how I feel, like you're reading my journal" and every single one of those became a buyer; do not let research become procrastination (exception: when you already know enough, write/call/sell).
- [Rule 10, p.127-132] Click moments for the 8020 Email Copy Course: (1) watching him write an email live on camera in 10-15 minutes while buyers spend 2 hours per email, so he now does it every time; (2) an emotional dog story email with no sales link that gets the most replies. Todd Herman found most buyers came from one old video about "grit" that he had stopped promoting.
- [Rule 11, p.133-136] Wants vs needs examples: sell "get your ex back" but deliver "get over your ex" (a "get over your ex" headline would hardly sell); limit: this is not bait and switch (customer wants a Ferrari, do not hand over a Honda); dating bootcamp sold pick-up lines, taught self-worth; "people don't want a vacuum, they want clean carpets, and friends impressed by them".
- [Rule 8, p.108-111, 168] First-line extras: the Double Your Sales Challenge opened with the question "What would it be worth to you to double your sales on just ONE of your funnels? Or one of your client's funnels?" (second line widens the audience); homework: check each funnel's first line, "does it feel like you CAN'T NOT keep reading?"; learn first lines by reading old sales letters or the first lines of thriller authors (Lee Child, Vince Flynn, James Patterson, Tom Clancy), in a physical bookstore; second most important part of a sales message is the offer/close; the opening line "I'm Ian from FIXT Water" in every video was a waste.
- [Rule 12, conclusion p.172] Example: a friend's embarrassing bowel-movement story email was his highest-performing email ever and sold a lot of supplements; embarrassing stories bring sales and long-term bond.
- [Rule 2, lesson 7, p.30] Lemonade lesson 7: ethically make customers aware of their shortcomings if the goal is to improve their life (golfer Ted loses balls, so sell extra balls); "scarcity and urgency whenever possible".
- [Rule 2 lesson 10, p.33] Celebrate wins: first $10k day in the water filter business was not celebrated ("could have been better"), which the author calls a mistake.

### Copy of Target Audience - Template (Dokument) (Course C) [L0395, undated]

Pains: cat never plays with its boring, mundane toys; toys stay still and do not stimulate; owner cannot get the cat to engage. Motivated away from: an unhappy cat, a cat that scratches the furniture, boredom, lack of engagement with the pet. Where: in the house. Trigger: when the cat is bored. Why it matters: an unhappy environment, and the owner's emotional connection to the cat. Deepest fears: house a mess, furniture ruined. (document, no timestamp, undated)
