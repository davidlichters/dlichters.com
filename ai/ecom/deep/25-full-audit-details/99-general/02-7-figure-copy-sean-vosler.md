## General (lessons whose topics have no chapter) (page 2 of 7)

### 7 Figure Copy - Sean Vosler (Dokument) (Course C) [L0196, 2020+] (continued)

- Framing research cited: more people support an economic policy when the employment rate is emphasised rather than the unemployment rate (Druckman 2001); 93% of PhD students registered early when a late-registration penalty fee was emphasised vs 67% when it was presented as an early-registration discount (Gaechter et al. 2009). Contextual savings examples: "A similar college level course costs well over $3,000, but today, for less than 10% of that price..."; "$2/day vs. $60". (doc p.69)
- Guiding question for all frames: "Am I presenting my information in the most attractive way?" Frames can be combined across pieces of marketing to appeal to different audience segments. (doc p.71)
- Loss-aversion illustration: someone who fears sharks gives up surfing although the chance of being killed by a shark is less than 1 in 264.1 million; security industries (insurance) rely heavily on loss aversion; age and experience influence loss aversion, but most people prefer the safe choice over a better one. When offering a discount, also emphasise that it will go away. (doc p.73)
- Loss-aversion priority: the primary question is how to emphasise the losses of NOT taking the action your message recommends; the secondary question is how to emphasise the gains of taking it. Tactic: provide lists of potential risks related to your topic, paired with the complementary benefits of avoiding them. (doc p.74)
- Staged FAQ answer structure: [validate the concern] -> [answer the objection] -> [reframe]. Example for a training course: Q "Doesn't this take a lot of time to implement before I see results?" A validates ("a very common concern"), answers with a "Day 1: Fast Action" quick-start section so results start on day 1, then reframes (a fast but temporary solution will fail; invest in a program built for the long haul). Typical miscalculations to address: time risk (how long until they benefit) and not understanding how good a deal they are getting. (doc p.75)
- Mere exposure limit: if someone already has a negative association with the subject, more exposure does not automatically make them view it more positively. (doc p.77)
- Instead of celebrity endorsements (usually out of reach), use influencers in your niche, paid by affiliate commission or a flat payment, to build repeated positive association. Use imagery to tell positive or negative association stories. (doc p.80)
- Guiding principle for message legacy: "people may forget what you said, but they won't forget how you made them feel"; repeated exposure alone is not enough, the message must also be impactful. Long-running slogan examples that show a winning message can run for decades: Got milk? (1993), Just do it (1988), What happens here, stays here (2004), Tastes great, less filling (1970s), Good to the last drop (1917), Melts in your mouth, not in your hands (1954), Breakfast of Champions (1927), "15 minutes could save 15% or more on car insurance". (doc p.81)
- The "21 impressions before a brand is remembered" figure is, by the author's own admission, "a statistic I remember but can't find the source for" (unsourced). (doc p.81)
- Bandwagon headline phrases also include "Tried & Tested by Dozens" and "Seen in Case after Case" (origin of "9 out of 10 dentists agree"). Bottom line: show they are not the first or last but one of many. (doc p.84)
- Choice-supportive bias, before the sale: show positive testimonials near the call to action; after the sale: positive language in follow-up and welcome emails and always thank the buyer (thank-you page); appreciation reduces refund rates. Buyer's remorse kicks in when there are not enough reasons to justify the purchase once emotions wane, especially at higher price points. (doc p.85)
- Pro-innovation feature-benefit-innovation example: "Our Soda Has Zero Sugar (feature) Which Keeps You Looking Great While Tasting Great (benefit)... We Do This Without the Use of Nasty Sugar Substitutes With Our Innovative Blend of Healthy Nutrients (innovation)." Headline example continues "...Unlocks Hidden Potential in Lazy People in 3 Simple to Follow Steps." (doc p.85)
- Lead by translation example: the Panasonic GH5s camera feature/benefit breakdown merges jargon features with plain-language benefits. (doc p.87, Copywriting Quickie #4)
- Nostalgia examples and effects: Coca-Cola calls back to its heritage at brand level and runs generation-specific nostalgia campaigns; vintage design suits food and beverage products (e.g. wine labels) and projects reliability; a later generation can appreciate a past generation's nostalgia (Polaroid cameras and vinyl records among millennials). Proven effects listed: increased social connectedness, improved mood, enhanced positive self-regard, existential meaning, psychological growth, likely increased sales. Don Draper's "Carousel" pitch (Mad Men) is the reference example. Light-hearted retro fonts, music and one-liners are "pandering" and a lazy use of nostalgia. For most brands the branding level should stay "here & now / future"; "new and innovative" frames should likely be prioritised there, with nostalgia used at campaign level. (doc p.90-93)
- Empathy is the foundation of persuasion: you cannot persuade someone effectively if they do not feel you understand them first. Types named: cognitive, emotional and somatic empathy. (doc p.100-101)
- Bullet points in copy collect key benefits (or features each with a clear benefit), not summaries; they reaffirm the headline's claims, guide the reader down the page and can contain open hooks and CTAs. For a webinar registration page whose goal is to entice, bullets can be pure curiosity hooks that open loops closed only by attending, without the feature/benefit/benefit-of-benefit wording. (doc p.102)
- Community Arbitrage details: copy the top 10 or so "most helpful" reviews into a document before highlighting. Example conversion: review statement "I've put my personal growth on the back burner for entirely too long." -> bullets "Feel like you've put your personal growth on the back burner for too long?" / "Our program helps you prioritize what really matters, without costing you all your free time." Rule: if it is true and a benefit to the reader, it is worth sharing; bullets can be short or longer. (doc p.103-105)
- Assembled page skeleton from Community Arbitrage: Headline ("A [Uniquely Powerful] Approach To [Overcoming Self Sabotage], And Why Many Have Embraced The Unique Strategy of 'Self Command'.") -> soft CTA / sub-headline that bridges to the bullets ("Register for Today's Free Training to Finally Learn...") -> 4 benefit bullets -> hard CTA ("Click Here to Register/Download/Buy & Unlock Your Full Potential"). (doc p.106)
- Bullet exercise (camera gimbal): read reviews of similar Amazon products and ask: which one feature catches the buyer's attention; how it makes life easier or improves their work quality vs not having it; how "making life easier" benefits them. Pure feature lists have their place, especially for physical products, but give no context on why each feature matters; benefit lists explain why but can still miss benefit pieces. (doc p.107)
- Grammar: get a functional grasp, and when in doubt hire an editor; recommended free reference: The Elements of Style by William Strunk (link.sean.co/elements). (doc p.108, Copywriting Quickie #5)
- Know-Like-Trust is achieved by teaching the prospect something that helps them get a desired result (email is ideal). Action item for Like/Know: ask "What are some relatable attributes of myself or my brand that would bring my reader into my world?" Challenge principle (top sales reps challenge the prospect's view of the problem): be the last vendor; example: a prospect asking for a traditional website (home, about, contact) is taught they really need an online lead-generation funnel adding X leads and $Y revenue per month. Benefit-of-benefit example: an infomercial hair-growth product ("Tired of being rejected at the bar because of your bad hairline?") is really selling confidence; ask "What are you really selling? Sell that, not your solution." (doc p.110-112)
- Know-Like-Trust-Challenge-Convert 5-email campaign templates, each ending with a cliffhanger that opens a loop to the next email: Email 1 "Know" (subject "Welcome, you're gonna love this..."): brainstorm who they are getting to know, why they should be excited, what about you helps their goals (case studies, mistakes you solved), when and why you started; CTA sells watching a 5-minute video, not the product; PS invites them to introduce themselves on the Facebook wall; PPS teases tomorrow's email and asks them to add your address to contacts. Email 2 "Like" (subject "the small business problem"): which people/organisations and principles you associate with, where you grew up and how it shaped your views, what you enjoy most about your business. Email 3 "Trust" (subject "Sleeping on the tennis court"): personal details, industry associations, how you helped others succeed, how your income improved your life; ask readers to share their thoughts. Email 4 "Challenge" (subject "Sarah's Story"): industry assumptions you proved wrong, what drove success contrary to popular belief, the #1 problem facing customers, unrecognised needs, the REAL problem they do not know about, how you will guide them; challenge readers to view their business "from 40,000 feet". Email 5 "Convert": the real solution, how finding it made you feel, measurable effects customers will experience, the number one reason to act now. (doc p.114-118)
- The A's of Influence framework (15 elements): Attention (fixate distracted eyes), Appeal (establish benefits), Acknowledgement (join the conversation in the audience's mind), Arousal (tap the emotional appeal), Angst (ratchet up, then remove the pain), Aspirations (where they are and want to be), Attitude (what it is now and what we want it to be), Authority (position and prove ourselves as trustworthy advisors), Ambiguity (identify and answer all objections), Ascension (guide through stages of growth), Affirm (take a stand for what you know is true), Associate (associate the brand with positive familiarity), Aggravate (aggravate and address built-in conflicts), Animate (give life to content), Action (present an offer they cannot refuse). Core sequence: capture attention through appeal (what's in it for them), influence attitude, then arouse to action. (doc p.135-136)
- Attention: many studies put the time to capture attention at under 5 seconds; Sol Stein observed no book browser went beyond page three before buying or putting the book down. Attention is relative to interest ("a goldfish has the attention span of a goldfish... until it's on a hook"); you keep attention "until you say something that allows their mind to wander" (Tucker Max). Study old and new ads (e.g. 1965 Northwest Airlines, Land Rover Defender, Heinz Ketchup, 1957 Mac's Resin Wax Coat, Nescafe) for attention techniques and metaphors. (doc p.137-139)
- Arousal: subtle teasing beats overt sex appeal. Describe universally understood events to attach feelings to the offer: humour -> joy; interaction with a desirable partner -> love, lust, companionship; removal of fear or anxiety -> remedies to common problems; reaching beyond one's limits -> self-esteem and actualisation; sadness or melancholy -> contrast with the positivity of the offer; story arcs (rags to riches, lost then found, search for "the one"). Hemingway's six-word story "For sale: baby shoes, never worn." shows few words can arouse strong emotion. "Make 'em cry, make 'em buy" (tears of sadness and joy equally welcome). (doc p.140-141)
- "Craft of Persuasion" / 5 Foundations framework (adapted from Blair Warren's "The One Sentence Persuasion Course"), each tied to an A of Influence: 1. Fan Their Flames (ascension), 2. Forgive Their Faults (aspirations), 3. Defeat Their Demons (aggravate), 4. Prove Their Presumptions (acknowledgement), 5. Attack Their Enemy (animate). Use them as a flexible framework: start at any point, address each in succession, or make one the primary focus depending on the piece's goal and audience. Related reading named: The 48 Laws of Power (Robert Greene), Story Wars (Jonah Sachs), How to Win Friends & Influence People (Dale Carnegie). Manipulation relies on false promises, unrealistic results and distorted facts; persuasion relies on skilful demonstration of the facts. (doc p.145-147)
- Fan Their Flames: help readers see the "gap" between where they are and where they want to be, inspire belief and give them the tools (your product) to close it; "When you advertise fire-extinguishers, open with the fire" (Ogilvy). With proper guidance the majority should reach their goal; if not, re-examine the product and refund those who do not "fill the gap". Persuasion is rooted in education. Ways to encourage positive emotion (affect heuristic, e.g. casinos are bright and colourful to make you happy): increase their understanding (especially about themselves), support passionate views, paint a more vivid picture of possibilities, and when using fear highlight the positive outcome of knowing ("You have a disease, but now that you know there's something you can do about it!"). Affect-based evaluations are quick and stronger when people lack resources to reflect, so a valid reason to act now heightens the effect (Henry Priest). Avoid shallow "you deserve it"/"just put your mind to it" hustle copy; inspire rebellion against self-doubt plus a realistic path. Do not start with praising the product; start with fanning their flames. (doc p.148-150)
- Forgive Their Faults: give readers "an out" by stating the problem is not them as a person but a lack of knowledge, false beliefs or an external enemy; use a forgiving tone, never accuse the reader. Bottled-water example: the misconception "it's just water" is answered by attaching real-world outcomes (energy, focus, better work and relationships, happiness) and forgiving them for not knowing the damage of sugary alternatives, shifting blame to "evil corporations". Pharma-ad pattern: dreary -> joy after the solution, with implied forgiveness because the treatment is "new" ("stop living with [condition]..."; "Most people don't realize there is now a new treatment option..."). Message as lighthouse: 1. define the storm, 2. define the light, 3. define the path. (doc p.154-158)
- Four-step forgiveness sequence (dating example): 1. acknowledge a negative/false belief ("So you think you're bad at dating..."); 2. give context why they are not alone in it; 3. forgive them for having it (they misattributed a situational issue, not leaving the house, to a value conclusion, being bad at dating; "THE REAL PROBLEM IS... you simply haven't put yourself out there"); 4. invite them to leave the false belief behind. Forgiveness builds trust, positions you as confidant and authority, and gives permission to forgive themselves. (doc p.162-163)
- Ego-morphing: purchases beyond utility confirm the buyer's self-image (status, confidence, power, sex); even the $20-watch buyer reinforces an ego trait (frugality). Examples: an ~$80,000 luxury bag bought as a gift, a $200,000 watch vs a $20 watch, Celine logo shopping bag $590, Kevin O'Leary's ~$80,000 FP Journe (one of 10) vs Marques Brownlee's utility smartwatch. Do not let your own judgment of buyers' reasons drive the copy. Ethics: ego-stroking is fine if you believe in the offer, offer a reasonable refund and make no false promises. Two ways to use it: (1) tailor imagery to the target's ego values (Jeep -> explorer; age-defying cream -> young and desirable); (2) combine ego-boosting with forgiving negative self-image errors. Personify a brand to imply attributes (Dos Equis "most interesting man in the world", Old Spice Guy). Ask: how does the offer alleviate negative self-image beliefs, and how does it enhance non-negative ones (vanity, esteem, social approval)? Be clear on 4 factors: what you sell, to whom, how the offer affects the buyer, and how the message affects the buyer (the 4th is where most copy is lacking). Reframe your product as "a new (better) way of doing things". (doc p.159-164)
- "Mistakes" research document, 5 steps: 1. Define the mistakes: write at least 3 harmful or misguided beliefs related to your offer (search "[keyword] mistakes" plus your niche on Medium.com), ideally ones you made or helped customers overcome; otherwise research articles, books, videos, or interview people who faced them. 2. Raise the stakes: bullet the real-world negative consequences and cascading effects of not addressing them. 3. The forgiveness: list internal and external reasons people fall for the mistakes (default: ignorance). 4. Grand illumination: list immediate benefits of fixing the mistake, then extrapolate to higher-level benefits; the final piece may focus on one key benefit with enough gravity. 5. What's next: list the steps to fix it, one of them your offer; blog posts can detail the steps, sales pages hint at them, emails may leave them to the next page. Worked example: Nick Wignall's "6 Subtle Habits That Are Sabotaging Your Happiness" (worry habit). (doc p.164-167)
- "Persuasion" comes from Latin persuadere, "complete advice": an argument is persuasive when complete. Empathise with both the pains and the joys of the audience; empathy without actionable, well-organised evidence erodes trust. When presenting information that contradicts core beliefs, expect push-back (cognitive dissonance) and therefore 1. present evidence for claims, 2. address likely dissonance reactions. (doc p.169-170)
- Defeat Their Demons: "demons" are the reader's rationalisations and false assumptions (e.g. "I'm not smart enough for this", or "I don't care about having white teeth" for a tooth-whitening seller) that act as objections to your evidence. Use phrases like "Now I know what you're thinking..." to manage objections actively, and an "admission of an alternative path" paragraph to pre-empt a likely conflicting opinion (objection handling). Simplified formula ("myth repellent"): 1. empathise with the reader's positive and negative views on the topic; 2. present a claim; 3. support it with evidence; 4. support the evidence by actively managing objections; 5. reach a reasonable, beneficial conclusion; 6. repeat. Complementary approach: the Socratic Method (bring them to a conclusion rather than handing them one; a Socratic circle is not a debate). (doc p.170-172)
- Three buckets of minds: open-minded, closed-minded, and ideological. Do not try to convince the ideological (bad-faith trolls; they treat any questioning as an enemy act); do not let them pressure you into silence. Closed-minded critics with legitimate concerns are valuable because they show where the message breaks down; tell them apart by their reaction to your answers: if they keep discussing they have real concerns (address them); if they attack you, your industry or people like you, they have no interest in learning. (doc p.173)
- Planning questions per element: Claim -> "What impactful revelation (insight) would our audience find provocative?"; Evidence -> "What facts, figures, studies, rhetorical questions and analogies support it?"; Objections -> "For each piece of evidence, what is the likely counter-argument and how do we proactively address it?" Goal: frame evidence so it engages the reader's critical thinking. (doc p.174)
- Six Socratic question frames for copy (after Christopher Pappas, "Socratic Questions in eLearning"), used rhetorically: 1. Clarification (ask the reader to consider why a claim or evidence is presented; e.g. "What is the number 1 entrepreneurship skill?" -> claim "Copywriting" -> "Why is it important to prioritise the skills we learn?"); 2. Probing assumptions (ask them to consider the path to a false conclusion: "everyone else does it", "the teacher said", "just the way it is"); 3. Probing reasons and evidence (follow up with how someone might reach the false belief and why that is understandable); 4. Analysing perspectives (invite them to consider how others reached a different conclusion; use with caution, can feel like an attack); 5. Probing consequences (list in story form the steps a false belief leads to vs the steps the right path leads to); 6. Questioning the question (invite them to think about why you asked; honestly admit your self-interest, then share the deeper reasons you care). (doc p.174-175)
- If you find you are wrong about something, revise your beliefs and adjust the argument. After finishing any marketing piece, revisit the introduction and make sure it contains a "claim of benefit" stating the main benefit of consuming the piece; weave "you"-focused benefit narrative throughout so readers never think "this doesn't apply to me". (doc p.176)
- Not every A of Influence needs to be in every piece; they should live across all pieces, built from one central "big idea". Rebranding should mean revisiting that central idea, not just a new logo or website. Pieces can focus on one demon, one forgiveness, or fan one flame then swing into an internal fault. Brute-force "flash in the pan" elements (urgency, scarcity, authority, social proof) without a larger message are manipulation with only short-term benefit; keep them second to the message. (doc p.177)
- Evidence rules: link to sources; share facts only to the needed depth, do not explain a statistic's methodology unless it matters to the argument; every claim needs at least one convincing piece of logic or factual information, and "the bigger the claim, the more evidence you'll need". Edutainment can carry more facts (examples: Kurzgesagt 10.5M subscribers, TED-Ed 10.3M, SmarterEveryDay 7.49M, Vsauce 15M); entertaining does not have to mean comical. (doc p.178)
- Attack Their Enemies: values define anti-values (courage vs cowardice, integrity vs corruption, wisdom vs wilful ignorance); align messaging with mutual values and disavow opposed ones. Three enemy types: 1. the struggle (problems your product reduces or removes); 2. the value battle; 3. the "boogie-man" enemy the marketer defines that the audience did not know to guard against. Enemies must relate to the offer; do not invent struggles. (doc p.181)
- Defining "struggle enemies": 1. list all features of the offer, even small ones; 2. connect each feature to a struggle it helps overcome; 3. write how you or a customer felt about the struggle; 4. rank struggles from most to least significant. Then show how each feature is a weapon against that enemy and that you understand the challenge. (doc p.182)
