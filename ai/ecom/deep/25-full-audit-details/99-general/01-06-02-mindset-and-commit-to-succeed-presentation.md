### 21-bullet-secrets (Dokument) (Course C) [L0068, undated]

- Credibility numbers given for Clayton Makepeace: attracted more than 3 million new customers and over 1 billion USD in sales for clients; Security Rare Coin from 3.5 million USD to over 120 million USD sales (3,400% increase) in a little over a year; quadrupled Blanchard & Company sales to over 115 million USD per year; over 1 million new paying customers for Phillips Publishing in just over 3 years; quadrupled the customer file of Safe Money Report. (2006, possibly outdated)
- It is rare to see a big direct mail package without at least 10-20 bullets/fascinations, often many more. (2006)
- In weak online bullet lists, often 3 out of every 4 bullets are mediocre; even one or two bad bullets make the prospect start skimming, and a few bad bullets alone can get the promotion tossed. (2006)
- Write tons of bullets; you will almost certainly end with at least a few very good headlines and probably 1-2 great ones. Example: the Bottom Line Personal control headline "How Doctors Stay Well While Treating Sick People All Day" began as a bullet, the most intriguing benefit-based one of many hundreds, and it hints at proof by referencing doctors. (2006)
- Boardroom popularized the "What NEVER" bullet; its blockbuster headline was "What never to eat on an airplane"; the fascinations from that piece helped build Boardroom into a direct mail titan (sold a free trial of Bottom Line Personal). Variations: "Why you should not ...", "The absolute worst ...", "What to avoid ...". (2006)
- Clayton puts his best bullets on the front and back cover; the back cover is packed with 5-10 teaser bullets that make the prospect open the piece to find the answers. (2006)
- Clayton's "horrifying alternatives" technique for pure benefit bullets: present what happens without the product (e.g. "Ages your eyes: free radicals damage your chromosomes ...", "Taxes your brain ..." citing New England Journal of Medicine research). (2006)
- Pure benefit bullets can also enhance the guarantee presentation (example: six bullets beginning "Avoid any brewing disasters ..."). (2006)
- When writing, keep the 21-formula report at hand; usually 2-3 formulas fit each piece of information; write one bullet per fitting formula, then pick the most intriguing until you have the number needed, and place them strategically throughout the copy. (2006)
- Gary Bencivenga is particularly fond of "If ... Then" statements as headlines and they produced very big winners for him; his bullets (bencivengabullets.com) promoted a 5,000 USD retirement seminar aimed at sophisticated "B/B+" writers. (2006)
- John Carlton's edgy promotion: bullets and fascinations made up about 80% of an eight-page letter, it beat the previous control and mailed profitably for over five years. (2006)
- Clayton (investment newsletters, supplements) uses fewer bullets than Boardroom/Rodale book packages; his bullets mainly sell premiums, sidebars, deck copy and back cover. (2006)

### 7 Figure Copy - Sean Vosler (Dokument) (Course C) [L0196, 2020+]

- Imitation Game headline modifiers, besides "End Result + Time Frame": (a) Fear of Loss, e.g. "The (Astonishingly Simple) Reason [Real Estate Agents] Often Ignore [Short Sales], and How Many Miss Out On 400% Higher Profits."; (b) Fear of Change, e.g. "New Scientific Approach is Teaching [Real Estate Agents] How to Unlock The Secret Power of [Short Sales]... Without Cannibalizing Their Core Real Estate Offers."; (c) Fear of Unknown + Hope, e.g. "Real Estate's Bleak Outlook In Three Charts and Why Short Sales Just Might Be The Simple Solution Agents Need." (doc p.34)
- Worked example of the Imitation Game: trending headline "The Man Who Perfected The Laugh Track" -> structure "The [noun] Who Perfected The [topic]" -> "The [Real Estate Agent] Who Perfected The [Short Sale]" -> expanded "Meet The [Real Estate Agent] Who Perfected The [Short Sale], and Learn How it 4x'd His Agency's Profits in 6 Months". The noun becomes the target audience, the topic becomes the product. It can help to write the imitated structure out without a subject first. (doc p.30-33)
- Headline starter list: "This is the closest we'll ever get to...", "Avoiding The Many Pitfalls of...", "An Elegant Demonstration Of...", "(testimonial) Frees Himself From...", "Understanding My Obsession With...", "Allow Me to Re-Introduce...", "The Unique Way XYZ Treats ZYX", "America's Obsession With XYZ - But...", "The Mysterious Life & Death of XYZ", "XYZ Is A Relatively New Problem". (doc p.35)
- Headline benefit modifier list: "... and How XYZ Saved More Time & Money By Making This Small Change.", "... and Why Now More Than Ever XYZ is Becoming More Common Place.", "... and What You Can Do To Change XYZ So You Can Enjoy More Freedom.", "... and What They Did to Increase Profits At The Same Time.", "... and Why You're Missing Out on XYZ if You're Not Embracing The Change." (doc p.35)
- A headline has "about the length of a hug" to grab attention and must accomplish at least one of six jobs: capture attention, inspire thought, confirm suspicion, create intrigue, promise powerfully, question reality; while also inspiring the reader to explore the rest of the content. (doc p.36)
- Structure of persuasive content after the headline (headline = thesis): introduction builds on the thesis, focuses on where the outcome ("promise land") takes the reader, answers "I know you're probably thinking..." questions, and builds credibility with facts, figures and quotes from reputable sources. Test whether an introduction is interesting by posting part of it on a social channel; low engagement means revamp it (more shocking, more revealing, or controversial by calling a popular view wrong). Keep an archive of articles, chapter-introduction screenshots and quotes. (doc p.37)
- "Even if" introduction example: headline "The exercise program that help even the most time crushed person lose weight!" -> intro "Even if you don't have hours to spend at the gym, this unique program can work for you..." leading into the "80/20 rule of fitness". (doc p.38)
- What follows the intro depends on the medium: an email can send readers straight to the offer with a "find out more" CTA; a sales page continues by sharing the outline of the training, focusing on the benefits of each step more than the techniques, ending with a CTA to buy the full system; a content piece (blog post, ebook, white paper) goes deeper into tactics but still ends with a CTA to learn about the offer. (doc p.38)
- Before choosing an objective, free-write everything about the subject, then throw the free-writing away, write a specific objective and start again. Objective template: "In this [email, campaign, sales page, webinar, ad] I want to convince my reader that [specific feature] is better for accomplishing [specific task] than their current method. To do this I will show specific evidence, share specific case studies, and appeal to xyz emotions." (doc p.40, Copywriting Quickie #1)
- Learning process for copy: 1. follow step-by-step instructions, 2. create something, 3. review it against examples and get feedback. (doc p.24)
- Copy builds trust by (1) posing an argument, (2) proving it correct, (3) delivering on the promised result. (doc p.15)
- AdvertSuite search modes are Keywords, Advertiser or Domain. Within keyword search: "Ad Text" gives the most relevant ads; "Comments" guarantees ads with engagement (ads with no comments do not show); "Landing Page Text" finds ads whose image does the talking and whose keywords sit on the landing page. When in doubt, search all three. Start keywords with your industry (e.g. Marketing), related topics (ads, copywriting, social media), your niche, and names of individuals in your industry. Use the word the ad itself would contain ("marketing", not "marketing services"). (doc p.44-46)
- AdvertSuite sorting: weigh engagement (likes, comments, shares) higher than time periods; "Longest Running" also signals success because most companies do not leave poorly converting ads running long. Start as broad as possible (keyword + sort only) before adding filters. (doc p.44-45)
- AdvertSuite filter guidance: Country, to restrict to your selling region or to compare how smart advertisers customise copy per region; "Ad Seen Between"/"Post Date Between", only for extremely broad keywords (e.g. "Holiday Special") or to compare last year's ads with this year's, useful for seasonal products; "Buttons", rarely used, to compare CTAs such as "learn more" vs "click here"; "Ad Position", Facebook newsfeed vs sidebar, which have different image/text specs, capture examples of each; Gender/Language/Age, to compare how advertisers change messaging for male vs female audiences and inspire separate ad sets; "Funnel" technology filter, not recommended; "E-Commerce" filter, useful to weed out service ads because those platforms are mostly physical products; "Casino" category, ignore unless in that industry. (doc p.45)
- Worked AdvertSuite search: Keywords > Ad Text "marketing", sorted by "Running Longest", with "Ad Seen Between" Jan 1 2019 to today (last 8 months) to see what works lately rather than the longest-running ads of all time. Save favourites with the star icon, but capture details in a third-party tool (a Google Doc works; Trello preferred). The "more details" icon links to the original ad. (doc p.46)
- Trello ad-research board setup: free version is enough; one column (list) per search keyword (author's own board is split by industry and medium); add a column via "+ Add another list", a card via "+ Add a card"; name each card "Page Title" + "Ad Type"; labels for platform (Facebook) and ad type (Newsfeed); put the AdvertSuite search settings in the card description; optional custom tags from ad keywords; comments for collaborators. Capture as much as possible because ad accounts get shut down or ads deleted; capture the long-form text only when you plan to study its structure. (doc p.47-48)
- Plagiarism rule of thumb: not using the same words and the exact structure of an ad is usually sufficient, especially for a similar product; consult a lawyer if unsure. (doc p.48)
- Remaining questions of the 30-question ad analysis worksheet (link.sean.co/ig-qws): what product/service is sold at the end of the funnel; its price point; what stoked engagement; what you would add to increase engagement; what effect the image had on engagement; what dream or desire the ad inspires; how the ad could improve; the thesis or main claim; what story it tells; whether it takes a contrarian point of view and how; which emotions it evokes and how; what lessons it teaches; what warnings it gives; what facts support its claims; what authority elements it contains; how it appeals to desires; what "enemy" it defines; what specific pains it names; what is entertaining or uniquely different; any rhyming or repetitive elements that drive home a point; what it associates itself with to raise perceived authority; what fears and hopes it brings to mind; in the audience's shoes, how it is effective and ineffective. Put the answers in each ad's Trello card. (doc p.49)
- Amazon R&D: sort the niche's book section by "best sellers" or "featured". The subtitle carries the promise (Smith Publicity: for nonfiction the subtitle is the most important element). Subtitle examples mapped to headline jobs: "A Counterintuitive Approach to Living a Good Life" = question reality; "Why We Do What We Do in Life and Business" = create intrigue; "How to Stop Doubting Your Greatness and Start Living an Awesome Life" = inspire thought; "The Secret to Love that Lasts" = promise powerfully. Worked adaptation: "A [Uniquely Powerful] Approach To [Making Millions Short Selling Homes], And Why Many Agents Are Missing Out Big Time." (doc p.53-56)
- "Grandma test" (attributed to Einstein): if you cannot explain it simply enough for your grandmother, you do not understand it well enough. The iPod succeeded by doing one thing flawlessly; aim for the same in copy. Often a large chunk of information can be summed up in one line. (doc p.58, Copywriting Quickie #3)
- There are as many as 180 known cognitive biases. Persuasion = to cause someone to act through reasoning or argument; manipulation = to influence cleverly, unfairly or unscrupulously. Use bias-based tactics only if you believe in the offer's real value, and stay truthful and transparent. (doc p.62)
- Choice overload: present the most common product options first with a way to display more; the term comes from Alvin Toffler's 1970 book Future Shock; images require less mental effort and increase perceived variety (Townsend & Kahn 2014). (doc p.65)
- Barnum-effect quiz example (charity for Australian fires): title "How 'Community Conscious' Are You? Your score may surprise you..."; three 1-10 scale questions (importance of supporting your community; how painful leaving your community would be; how emotionally affected by news of the fires); set up 3 groups in Typeform (less interested, interested, very interested in community) and show each a different message; the "very interested" message congratulates them, then segues into a CTA with a dollar-for-dollar donor match "today only". Most respondents land in the "very interested" group if questions are posed correctly. (doc p.67)
